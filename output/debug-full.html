How to use this book

This is a working manual, not a book you read once and shelve. It's built to sit open on a second monitor while you do the thing.

If you have no clients yet

Read it front to back, in order. The chapters are sequenced the way the work actually happens, and skipping ahead to the automation chapters before you've talked to a single business owner is the most common way people waste a year.

Do the Do This Now box at the end of each chapter before moving on. Each one is a single action sized to finish in under 45 minutes. There are eighteen of them. If you do all eighteen, you will have chosen a market, built an offer, priced it, written your outreach, run calls, and stood up delivery — in that order.

If you already have clients

Use the stage tag at the top of each chapter. Every chapter belongs to one of seven stages, and the stage tells you what problem it solves. If your problem is that nobody replies to your outreach, go to VOLUME. If your problem is that clients leave after two months, go to PROOF. Read what you need.

The asset vault

Every script, template, worksheet, and checklist in this book also exists as a standalone file you can fill in. They're listed in the back, indexed by chapter. Use those rather than retyping out of the book.

A note on the numbers

Where a number appears in this book, it is one of two things and it will always say which: something measured in the author's own business, or an illustrative example built to show how the arithmetic works. There is no third category. Nothing here is a benchmark you should measure yourself against, because a benchmark from someone else's market is worse than no benchmark at all.

Track your own numbers from week one. By month three they will be the only numbers that matter.

The Domino System

Seven stages. The first client is the hardest thing you will do. Each one after costs less — because each one tips the next.

1 · LANE — Choose one vertical and one radius you can physically drive to. Written down, today.
2 · OFFER — Build one offer at one price, sold in booked appointments, not vague "leads."
3 · VOLUME — A daily touch count you hit whether or not you feel like it, tracked in a pipeline.
4 · CLOSE — Diagnose before you pitch. End the call with a decision, not a "let me think."
5 · BUILD — Register for messaging first, then stand up the machine. Drive hard at the activation point.
6 · PROOF — Report in the client's language: appointments and jobs won, not impressions.
7 · TIP — Ask at the moment the result lands. The referral re-enters at VOLUME, already half closed.

Stages 1 and 2 you do once and revisit. Stages 3 through 7 are the loop you live in. The dotted line is the whole point of this book: work done at stage 7 shows up as easier work at stage 3, which is why the tenth client costs a fraction of what the first one did.

1 The Domino System · The whole idea

The First Domino

By the end of this chapter you'll understand why the first client costs everything and the tenth costs almost nothing — and you'll stop reading the curve backwards.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words The day you got your first paying client. Where you were standing. What time of year — in season or out. Who they were and what they sell. What you charged. What you actually said. And the honest part: did you know how to do the work yet when they said yes?

I want to tell you about the part nobody puts in the ad.

Everybody selling you an agency course shows you month eighteen. The dashboard with eleven clients on it. The calendar with six calls booked before lunch. What they don't show you is month one, when you sent forty messages and got two replies and both of them were no.

Here's what I know now that I didn't know then. Those two curves are the same curve. You're just looking at it from the wrong end.

The principle

The principle

The first client is the hardest thing you will ever do in this business. Every client after that costs less than the one before it.

That's it. That's the whole book in one sentence, and everything else in here is a method for surviving long enough to prove it to yourself.

Why most people get this backwards

Ask somebody who has never run an agency what the hard part is, and they'll tell you it's scaling. Going from one client to ten. Hiring. Systems. The stuff that sounds hard because it involves spreadsheets.

So they brace for the wrong thing. They spend three months building a website, picking a logo, registering an LLC, and watching videos about how to scale — all before a single business owner has ever heard their name. Then they send their first batch of messages, get ignored, and conclude they picked the wrong niche.

They didn't pick the wrong niche. They just met the hardest part of the job and thought it was a sign.

Think about what you're actually carrying when you go after client number one. No case study. No screenshot of a result. No name they recognize. No one who can vouch for you. Nothing but a claim, from a stranger, about a thing they've probably already been pitched on twice this month.

Now think about what you're carrying when you go after client number ten. A client in their industry who will take a phone call and say you're good. Numbers from a real account. A story about the exact problem this person has, because you fixed it in April for someone two towns over.

Same you. Same offer. Completely different weight.

Client one is a cold pitch from a nobody. Client ten is a warm introduction from someone they trust. You are not doing the same job twice.

The system

This is why the book is built the way it is.

The Domino System has seven stages, and they run in a loop. You'll see the full map on the page before this chapter, and every chapter from here tells you which stage it belongs to.

Stages one and two you do once, then revisit occasionally. LANE is choosing a market you can physically drive to. OFFER is building one thing you sell at one price.

Stages three through seven are the loop you actually live in:

VOLUME is outreach — a daily number you hit whether or not you feel like it. CLOSE is turning a conversation into a signature. BUILD is standing up the machine that delivers. PROOF is making the result visible to the person paying for it. TIP is turning that result into the next client.

The dotted line on the map matters more than the boxes. It runs from stage seven back to stage three. A referred prospect re-enters at VOLUME — but they arrive already half closed, because somebody they trust already told them you're worth talking to.

That's the domino. You don't get a shortcut on the first one. You earn a shortcut on every one after.

The math of it, in hours

Forget dollars for a second, because if you're starting out your real currency is hours.

Say client one takes you 60 hours of outreach, follow-up, calls, and rework to land. That's an illustrative number, not a promise — plug in your own once you have it.

Client two comes partly warm, because client one has now said something nice to somebody. Maybe it takes 40 hours. Client three, 25. By client six you have proof, a script that works, and a referral or two arriving without you asking.

Nothing about you got smarter between client one and client six. You just stopped starting from zero every time.

The mistake is to look at that 60-hour first client and decide the whole business costs 60 hours per client. It doesn't. That's the entry fee, paid once.

Failure modes

You quit in the flat part.
Write down, today, the number of outreach attempts you're willing to make before you allow yourself to conclude the method is broken. Make it a big number and make it before you're discouraged. Deciding that number while you feel fine is the only way it survives the week you feel terrible.
You rebuild the plan instead of running it.
Changing your niche, your offer, and your script in the same week means you learn nothing from any of them. Change one thing, run enough volume to see whether it moved, then change the next.
You treat the first client like the last one you'll ever get.
Underpricing to get a yes feels smart at the time and sets a ceiling you'll fight for a year. Charge what the work is worth even when you're nervous — Chapter 5 shows you how to figure out what that is.
You get client one and stop doing outreach.
Delivery eats the whole week, outreach stops, and sixty days later you're back at zero with one client and no pipeline. The daily number in Chapter 2 does not pause because you got busy.

The metric

What to track in this stage

Attempts to first yes. One number: how many businesses you contacted before one agreed to pay you. Count every attempt, including the ones that went nowhere.

You only get to measure it once, so write it down when it happens. It becomes the number you compare everything else against — and the number you give the next person who tells you this doesn't work.

What "good" looks like: there isn't a published figure worth trusting here, and anyone who quotes you one is guessing. Your number depends on your market, your channel, and how bad your first fifty messages are. Track it, beat it next time.

Do this now

20 minutes Open a note on your phone and write two things.

First: the number of outreach attempts you'll make before you're allowed to decide this doesn't work. Pick something that would embarrass you to fall short of.

Second: the date you're writing it.

That's the whole exercise. You're making a promise to yourself while you're still fresh, because the version of you in week five is going to want to renegotiate, and this note is what he has to argue with.

2 The Domino System · Stage 3 — Volume

It's a Numbers Game

By the end of this chapter you'll have a daily number you can hit without motivation, and you'll understand why the return shows up after the volume instead of during it.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words The grind before the first yes. How long it actually took. Roughly how many businesses you contacted. What a day looked like — lifts, class, practice, film, and where the agency work physically fit. And the lowest point: the day it felt stupid and you thought about dropping it.

I say this to everyone who asks me how I did it, and most of them look disappointed.

It's a numbers game. Put the work in, and eventually enough comes back that it feels worth it.

That's not the answer people want. People want a script. They want the specific message that made a landscaper reply. And I get it — a script feels like a thing you can hold. A number feels like being told to work harder, which is what your uncle says when he doesn't know what your job is.

But I mean something specific by it, and the specific version is worth more than any script I could hand you.

The principle

The principle

The return arrives after the volume, not during it. Most people quit while the math is still working.

Why most people get this wrong

Here's the trap, and almost everyone walks into it.

You send fifty messages in week one. You get three replies and no calls. You conclude the message is broken. So you rewrite it. Week two, you send fifty of the new one. Two replies, one call, no close. You conclude the offer is broken. So you change the offer. Week three, new niche.

Three weeks in, you have run three experiments, learned nothing from any of them, and you feel like you worked hard. You did work hard. You just spent all of it re-deciding instead of doing.

The reason this feels right is that it looks like optimization. It looks like you're being smart and iterating. But you can't read a signal out of fifty attempts. Fifty is noise. Whatever result you got from those fifty, you'd get a meaningfully different result from the next fifty with the exact same message — that's just variance.

Changing your message every week isn't iteration. It's a way of feeling productive while never finding out whether anything works.

The other half of the trap is emotional. A bad week feels like evidence. It isn't. It's a sample. And you are the worst possible judge of your own sample size, because you're the one absorbing every no.

The system

1. Pick a daily number, not a weekly one

Weekly targets get back-loaded. You tell yourself you'll do 100 this week, do 12 by Thursday, and then either cram 88 into Friday badly or quietly move the goal.

Daily numbers don't let you hide. You either hit today's or you didn't, and you find out in 24 hours instead of seven days.

Make it a number of attempts, not outcomes. You don't control replies. You control sends.

2. Set it at the level you can hit on your worst day

This is the part people get backwards. They set the number for their best day — the Sunday afternoon with nothing else on. Then Tuesday happens, with a full day of work or class and something going wrong, and they miss it. Miss it three times and the number stops meaning anything.

Set it for the worst realistic day you'll have this month. If that's 15, it's 15. A 15 you hit every single day beats a 40 you hit twice a week, because the 15 compounds and the 40 turns into a story about how you're "not consistent."

You can always do more on a good day. Extra is extra. The floor is the thing you defend.

3. Put it in a fixed block, at a fixed time

Anything that doesn't have a time doesn't happen. Pick the block and defend it like a practice you can't skip.

The best block is usually early, before the day starts making demands. Second best is whatever gap actually exists in your real schedule — not the one you wish you had.

4. Track attempts, replies, and conversations. Three numbers.

Not in your head. Your head lies about this, in both directions — you'll feel like you did tons of outreach on a week you did four, and you'll feel like you're failing on a week you did great.

Attempts is what you sent. Replies is anyone who wrote back, including the rude ones. Conversations is anyone who engaged past the first exchange.

Chapter 7 shows you how to track this properly inside a pipeline. For this week, a note on your phone with three tally marks is enough.

5. Do not change anything for the first 300 attempts

Three hundred is not a magic number. It's just big enough that the result means something and small enough to reach in a few weeks.

Run the same message, to the same kind of business, through the same channel, 300 times. Then look at the three numbers and change one thing.

This is the hardest instruction in the chapter and the one that separates people who build something from people who have a folder of half-tested ideas.

Before you send a single text

If any of your outreach happens over SMS, you need A2P 10DLC registration first. Since February 2025, US carriers block unregistered application-to-person traffic outright — your messages don't get delivered slowly, they don't get delivered at all. Registration takes days, not minutes. Chapter 11 walks the whole process. Start it before you need it.

Failure modes

You set the number for the person you wish you were.
Cut it in half. Hit the smaller number for two straight weeks before you raise it. Consistency at a low number beats intensity at a high one, because only one of them survives a bad Tuesday.
You count outcomes instead of attempts.
Targets like "book two calls today" put your daily win in someone else's hands. Track outcomes as a result, never as the goal. The goal is always something you can do alone.
You stop the day you land a client.
This is the most expensive one in the book. Delivery expands to eat the week, outreach stops, and two months later you have one client and an empty pipeline. Protect the block on delivery weeks too, even at a reduced number.
You rewrite the message after a bad morning.
Log the bad morning and keep sending. The 300 rule exists precisely to protect you from the version of yourself who has just been ignored eleven times before breakfast.
You do the volume but never look at the numbers.
Volume without measurement is just effort. Total the three columns every Sunday — it takes four minutes and it's the only way you'll ever know which change actually helped.

The metric

What to track in this stage

Reply rate — replies divided by attempts. This tells you whether your message and your targeting are working. It moves when you change who you contact or what you say first.

Conversation rate — conversations divided by attempts. This tells you whether the interest is real. A high reply rate with a low conversation rate usually means your opener is interesting but your offer isn't landing.

What "good" looks like: your own last 300 attempts. That's the only benchmark that means anything. Published reply-rate figures come from other people's markets, channels, and lists, and measuring yourself against them will either scare you off something that's working or make you comfortable with something that isn't.

Do this now

30 minutes Three things, in order.

One. Look at your actual calendar for the next seven days — the real one, with class and work and everything else in it. Find the block that exists every single day. Write the time down.

Two. Pick the number you could hit inside that block on your worst day this month. Be honest and go low.

Three. Set a recurring calendar event for that block, every day, starting tomorrow. Title it with the number. Not "outreach" — the actual number, so you see it before you open it.

Then do tomorrow's.

3 The Domino System · Stage 1 — Lane

Pick a Lane You Can Drive To

By the end of this chapter you'll have one vertical and one radius written down — chosen with a scorecard in an afternoon instead of agonized over for six months.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words How you actually ended up serving the verticals you serve. Did you sit down and choose landscaping, or did a landscaper say yes first and the rest followed? Be honest about which — the honest version is more useful to the reader than the tidy one.

Niche selection is where more beginners stall out than anywhere else in this business, and it's not close.

Not because it's hard. Because it's the last decision you can make without talking to anybody. Once you pick, the next thing on the list is contacting strangers, and there is an enormous psychological pull toward staying in the part where you're still "planning."

I've watched people spend eleven weeks on this. Spreadsheets. Reddit threads. Three different YouTube frameworks. At the end of eleven weeks they had a niche they weren't confident in and eleven fewer weeks.

The principle

The principle

Pick a lane you can drive to in twenty minutes, where one extra booked job obviously covers your fee. Then start. The lane sharpens with data you don't have yet.

Why most people get this wrong

Two mistakes, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is picking a niche you can't reach. Somebody reads that med spas have high margins, so they decide they serve med spas — nationally. Now they're a stranger on the internet competing with fifty other strangers on the internet, with no reason for anyone to pick them.

The second is refusing to pick at all. "I'll work with any local business." That sounds flexible and it's actually the most expensive choice on the board, because every conversation starts from scratch. You can't reuse a single sentence, and you never build the thing that makes client three easier than client one.

There's a third thing worth naming, and it contradicts most advice you'll hear.

You will probably not choose your niche. Your niche will choose you — whoever says yes first. That is a completely acceptable way to start, as long as you notice it happening.

Confirm before publication This section asserts that Isaiah's lane emerged rather than being chosen up front, based on "one client led to another." If he actually picked deliberately, this whole section should be cut, not softened — the value here is honesty, and a hedged version is worse than either true version.

The reason this matters: if you believe you must pick correctly before starting, then picking becomes a high-stakes decision and you'll avoid it. If you believe you're picking a direction that will sharpen once real businesses start replying, you'll pick this afternoon.

Both are true. The direction matters. The precision doesn't, yet.

The system

The six screens

Four of these come from Alex Hormozi's criteria for choosing an audience — a growing market, one with money, one that's easy to target, one in pain. They're good and I use them. The last two are mine, and for a beginner working a local market they matter more than the first four.

1. Growing. Are there more of these businesses this year than last? A shrinking market means you're fighting for a smaller pot every quarter.

2. Can afford you. What does one job pay them? A business where the average job is $180 cannot pay you what a business where the average job is $4,000 can, no matter how much they like you.

3. Easy to find. Can you build a list of 200 of them in an hour? If they're not on Google Maps, not in a directory, and not in a trade association, you'll spend your time on list-building instead of outreach.

4. In pain. Do they visibly need more customers? Competition is a good sign here, not a bad one. If nobody's advertising in a vertical, that's often because it doesn't work.

5. Can you reach the owner directly? This is the screen nobody talks about and it decides everything early on. In a two-person landscaping company, the owner answers the phone. In a regional chain, you'll spend four weeks getting past a gatekeeper to a marketing manager who has a vendor already. Early on, you want businesses small enough that the person who can say yes is the person who picks up.

6. Does one extra job obviously pay your fee? Do the arithmetic out loud, in their terms. If a roofer closes one in three estimates and a roof is $9,000 at 30% margin, then one extra estimate a month is roughly $900 in margin. If you're charging $500 a month, the math is not a debate — it's arithmetic they can do in their head while you're talking. If you have to build a spreadsheet to justify your fee, pick a different vertical.

The radius

Set it at twenty to thirty minutes of driving, then stop expanding.

Density beats size. Two hundred qualifying businesses inside twenty minutes is worth more than two thousand spread across the state, for four reasons that compound:

You can show up in person, which almost nobody does anymore. They know the streets you name. One client's referral is likely to be another business in the same radius. And your case study is about a business their customers actually use.

The instinct is to widen the radius when outreach is slow. Resist it. A slow week inside a tight radius is a message problem or a volume problem, and widening the map hides both.

Failure modes

You pick by margin alone.
High-margin verticals are usually the ones every agency already targets. Weight reachability and the fee arithmetic above margin — a slightly less profitable vertical where the owner picks up the phone will make you more money in year one.
You widen the radius when it gets quiet.
Hold the radius and fix the message instead. Widening the map converts a diagnosable problem into an undiagnosable one.
You pick something you can't get access to.
Before committing, spend twenty minutes trying to reach three owners in the vertical. If you can't get a human in three tries, that's your answer, and you found it in twenty minutes instead of two months.
You change lanes after four bad weeks.
Four bad weeks is not a verdict on a market, it's a verdict on 200 attempts. Apply the 300-attempt rule from Chapter 2 before you conclude anything about the vertical.
You pick a vertical with no seasonality awareness.
Landscaping in January and snow removal in July are different businesses. Check when your vertical's money actually moves, and plan outreach to arrive a month before their season starts, not during it.

The metric

What to track in this stage

Qualifying businesses inside your radius. One number, counted once. Open a map, search your vertical, count what's inside the boundary you drew.

If the answer is under 100, either the radius is too tight or the vertical is too narrow — widen one of them, once, and recount.

If the answer is over 500, you have plenty and the constraint is your daily number, not your market.

What "good" looks like: enough to sustain your daily number for six months without repeating yourself. At 20 attempts a day, five days a week, that's roughly 400 businesses with room for follow-ups. Do that arithmetic with your own number.

Do this now

45 minutes Open a map. Draw a twenty-minute radius around where you live.

Pick three service verticals inside it — the ones you'd guess first are fine. Score each one against the seven rows in the table above. Don't research; use what you already believe. The scorecard's job is to make you decide, not to be right.

Take the winner. Write one sentence on a note you'll see tomorrow:

"I sell to [vertical] within [radius]."

Then open the map again and count how many of them there are. That number is your market, and you now have one.
