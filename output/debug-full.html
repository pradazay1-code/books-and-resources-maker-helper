The Domino System

Seven stages. The first client is the hardest thing you will do. Each one after costs less — because each one tips the next.

1 · LANE — Choose one vertical and one radius you can physically drive to. Written down, today.
2 · OFFER — Build one offer at one price, sold in booked appointments, not vague "leads."
3 · VOLUME — A daily touch count you hit whether or not you feel like it, tracked in a pipeline.
4 · CLOSE — Diagnose before you pitch. End the call with a decision, not a "let me think."

1 The Domino System · The whole idea

The First Domino

By the end of this chapter you'll understand why the first client costs everything and the tenth costs almost nothing — and you'll stop reading the curve backwards.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words The day you got your first paying client. Where you were standing. What time of year — in season or out. Who they were and what they sell. What you charged. What you actually said. And the honest part: did you know how to do the work yet when they said yes?

I want to tell you about the part nobody puts in the ad.

Everybody selling you an agency course shows you month eighteen. The dashboard with eleven clients on it. The calendar with six calls booked before lunch. What they don't show you is month one, when you sent forty messages and got two replies and both of them were no.

Here's what I know now that I didn't know then. Those two curves are the same curve. You're just looking at it from the wrong end.

The principle

The principle

The first client is the hardest thing you will ever do in this business. Every client after that costs less than the one before it.

That's it. That's the whole book in one sentence, and everything else in here is a method for surviving long enough to prove it to yourself.

Why most people get this backwards

Ask somebody who has never run an agency what the hard part is, and they'll tell you it's scaling. Going from one client to ten. Hiring. Systems. The stuff that sounds hard because it involves spreadsheets.

So they brace for the wrong thing. They spend three months building a website, picking a logo, registering an LLC, and watching videos about how to scale — all before a single business owner has ever heard their name. Then they send their first batch of messages, get ignored, and conclude they picked the wrong niche.

They didn't pick the wrong niche. They just met the hardest part of the job and thought it was a sign.

Think about what you're actually carrying when you go after client number one. No case study. No screenshot of a result. No name they recognize. No one who can vouch for you. Nothing but a claim, from a stranger, about a thing they've probably already been pitched on twice this month.

Now think about what you're carrying when you go after client number ten. A client in their industry who will take a phone call and say you're good. Numbers from a real account. A story about the exact problem this person has, because you fixed it in April for someone two towns over.

Same you. Same offer. Completely different weight.

Client one is a cold pitch from a nobody. Client ten is a warm introduction from someone they trust. You are not doing the same job twice.

The system

This is why the book is built the way it is.

The Domino System has seven stages, and they run in a loop. You'll see the full map on the page before this chapter, and every chapter from here tells you which stage it belongs to.

Stages one and two you do once, then revisit occasionally. LANE is choosing a market you can physically drive to. OFFER is building one thing you sell at one price.

Stages three through seven are the loop you actually live in:

VOLUME is outreach — a daily number you hit whether or not you feel like it. CLOSE is turning a conversation into a signature. BUILD is standing up the machine that delivers. PROOF is making the result visible to the person paying for it. TIP is turning that result into the next client.

The dotted line on the map matters more than the boxes. It runs from stage seven back to stage three. A referred prospect re-enters at VOLUME — but they arrive already half closed, because somebody they trust already told them you're worth talking to.

That's the domino. You don't get a shortcut on the first one. You earn a shortcut on every one after.

The math of it, in hours

Forget dollars for a second, because if you're starting out your real currency is hours.

Say client one takes you 60 hours of outreach, follow-up, calls, and rework to land. That's an illustrative number, not a promise — plug in your own once you have it.

Client two comes partly warm, because client one has now said something nice to somebody. Maybe it takes 40 hours. Client three, 25. By client six you have proof, a script that works, and a referral or two arriving without you asking.

Nothing about you got smarter between client one and client six. You just stopped starting from zero every time.

The mistake is to look at that 60-hour first client and decide the whole business costs 60 hours per client. It doesn't. That's the entry fee, paid once.

Failure modes

You quit in the flat part.
Write down, today, the number of outreach attempts you're willing to make before you allow yourself to conclude the method is broken. Make it a big number and make it before you're discouraged. Deciding that number while you feel fine is the only way it survives the week you feel terrible.
You rebuild the plan instead of running it.
Changing your niche, your offer, and your script in the same week means you learn nothing from any of them. Change one thing, run enough volume to see whether it moved, then change the next.
You treat the first client like the last one you'll ever get.
Underpricing to get a yes feels smart at the time and sets a ceiling you'll fight for a year. Charge what the work is worth even when you're nervous — Chapter 5 shows you how to figure out what that is.
You get client one and stop doing outreach.
Delivery eats the whole week, outreach stops, and sixty days later you're back at zero with one client and no pipeline. The daily number in Chapter 2 does not pause because you got busy.

The metric

What to track in this stage

Attempts to first yes. One number: how many businesses you contacted before one agreed to pay you. Count every attempt, including the ones that went nowhere.

You only get to measure it once, so write it down when it happens. It becomes the number you compare everything else against — and the number you give the next person who tells you this doesn't work.

What "good" looks like: there isn't a published figure worth trusting here, and anyone who quotes you one is guessing. Your number depends on your market, your channel, and how bad your first fifty messages are. Track it, beat it next time.

Do this now

20 minutes Open a note on your phone and write two things.

First: the number of outreach attempts you'll make before you're allowed to decide this doesn't work. Pick something that would embarrass you to fall short of.

Second: the date you're writing it.

That's the whole exercise. You're making a promise to yourself while you're still fresh, because the version of you in week five is going to want to renegotiate, and this note is what he has to argue with.

2 The Domino System · Stage 3 — Volume

It's a Numbers Game

By the end of this chapter you'll have a daily number you can hit without motivation, and you'll understand why the return shows up after the volume instead of during it.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words The grind before the first yes. How long it actually took. Roughly how many businesses you contacted. What a day looked like — lifts, class, practice, film, and where the agency work physically fit. And the lowest point: the day it felt stupid and you thought about dropping it.

I say this to everyone who asks me how I did it, and most of them look disappointed.

It's a numbers game. Put the work in, and eventually enough comes back that it feels worth it.

That's not the answer people want. People want a script. They want the specific message that made a landscaper reply. And I get it — a script feels like a thing you can hold. A number feels like being told to work harder, which is what your uncle says when he doesn't know what your job is.

But I mean something specific by it, and the specific version is worth more than any script I could hand you.

The principle

The principle

The return arrives after the volume, not during it. Most people quit while the math is still working.

Why most people get this wrong

Here's the trap, and almost everyone walks into it.

You send fifty messages in week one. You get three replies and no calls. You conclude the message is broken. So you rewrite it. Week two, you send fifty of the new one. Two replies, one call, no close. You conclude the offer is broken. So you change the offer. Week three, new niche.

Three weeks in, you have run three experiments, learned nothing from any of them, and you feel like you worked hard. You did work hard. You just spent all of it re-deciding instead of doing.

The reason this feels right is that it looks like optimization. It looks like you're being smart and iterating. But you can't read a signal out of fifty attempts. Fifty is noise. Whatever result you got from those fifty, you'd get a meaningfully different result from the next fifty with the exact same message — that's just variance.

Changing your message every week isn't iteration. It's a way of feeling productive while never finding out whether anything works.

The other half of the trap is emotional. A bad week feels like evidence. It isn't. It's a sample. And you are the worst possible judge of your own sample size, because you're the one absorbing every no.

The system

1. Pick a daily number, not a weekly one

Weekly targets get back-loaded. You tell yourself you'll do 100 this week, do 12 by Thursday, and then either cram 88 into Friday badly or quietly move the goal.

Daily numbers don't let you hide. You either hit today's or you didn't, and you find out in 24 hours instead of seven days.

Make it a number of attempts, not outcomes. You don't control replies. You control sends.

2. Set it at the level you can hit on your worst day

This is the part people get backwards. They set the number for their best day — the Sunday afternoon with nothing else on. Then Tuesday happens, with a full day of work or class and something going wrong, and they miss it. Miss it three times and the number stops meaning anything.

Set it for the worst realistic day you'll have this month. If that's 15, it's 15. A 15 you hit every single day beats a 40 you hit twice a week, because the 15 compounds and the 40 turns into a story about how you're "not consistent."

You can always do more on a good day. Extra is extra. The floor is the thing you defend.

3. Put it in a fixed block, at a fixed time

Anything that doesn't have a time doesn't happen. Pick the block and defend it like a practice you can't skip.

The best block is usually early, before the day starts making demands. Second best is whatever gap actually exists in your real schedule — not the one you wish you had.

4. Track attempts, replies, and conversations. Three numbers.

Not in your head. Your head lies about this, in both directions — you'll feel like you did tons of outreach on a week you did four, and you'll feel like you're failing on a week you did great.

Attempts is what you sent. Replies is anyone who wrote back, including the rude ones. Conversations is anyone who engaged past the first exchange.

Chapter 7 shows you how to track this properly inside a pipeline. For this week, a note on your phone with three tally marks is enough.

5. Do not change anything for the first 300 attempts

Three hundred is not a magic number. It's just big enough that the result means something and small enough to reach in a few weeks.

Run the same message, to the same kind of business, through the same channel, 300 times. Then look at the three numbers and change one thing.

This is the hardest instruction in the chapter and the one that separates people who build something from people who have a folder of half-tested ideas.

Before you send a single text

If any of your outreach happens over SMS, you need A2P 10DLC registration first. Since February 2025, US carriers block unregistered application-to-person traffic outright — your messages don't get delivered slowly, they don't get delivered at all. Registration takes days, not minutes. Chapter 11 walks the whole process. Start it before you need it.

Failure modes

You set the number for the person you wish you were.
Cut it in half. Hit the smaller number for two straight weeks before you raise it. Consistency at a low number beats intensity at a high one, because only one of them survives a bad Tuesday.
You count outcomes instead of attempts.
Targets like "book two calls today" put your daily win in someone else's hands. Track outcomes as a result, never as the goal. The goal is always something you can do alone.
You stop the day you land a client.
This is the most expensive one in the book. Delivery expands to eat the week, outreach stops, and two months later you have one client and an empty pipeline. Protect the block on delivery weeks too, even at a reduced number.
You rewrite the message after a bad morning.
Log the bad morning and keep sending. The 300 rule exists precisely to protect you from the version of yourself who has just been ignored eleven times before breakfast.
You do the volume but never look at the numbers.
Volume without measurement is just effort. Total the three columns every Sunday — it takes four minutes and it's the only way you'll ever know which change actually helped.

The metric

What to track in this stage

Reply rate — replies divided by attempts. This tells you whether your message and your targeting are working. It moves when you change who you contact or what you say first.

Conversation rate — conversations divided by attempts. This tells you whether the interest is real. A high reply rate with a low conversation rate usually means your opener is interesting but your offer isn't landing.

What "good" looks like: your own last 300 attempts. That's the only benchmark that means anything. Published reply-rate figures come from other people's markets, channels, and lists, and measuring yourself against them will either scare you off something that's working or make you comfortable with something that isn't.

Do this now

30 minutes Three things, in order.

One. Look at your actual calendar for the next seven days — the real one, with class and work and everything else in it. Find the block that exists every single day. Write the time down.

Two. Pick the number you could hit inside that block on your worst day this month. Be honest and go low.

Three. Set a recurring calendar event for that block, every day, starting tomorrow. Title it with the number. Not "outreach" — the actual number, so you see it before you open it.

Then do tomorrow's.

3 The Domino System · Stage 1 — Lane

Pick a Lane You Can Drive To

By the end of this chapter you'll have one vertical and one radius written down — chosen with a scorecard in an afternoon instead of agonized over for six months.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words How you actually ended up serving the verticals you serve. Did you sit down and choose landscaping, or did a landscaper say yes first and the rest followed? Be honest about which — the honest version is more useful to the reader than the tidy one.

Niche selection is where more beginners stall out than anywhere else in this business, and it's not close.

Not because it's hard. Because it's the last decision you can make without talking to anybody. Once you pick, the next thing on the list is contacting strangers, and there is an enormous psychological pull toward staying in the part where you're still "planning."

I've watched people spend eleven weeks on this. Spreadsheets. Reddit threads. Three different YouTube frameworks. At the end of eleven weeks they had a niche they weren't confident in and eleven fewer weeks.

The principle

The principle

Pick a lane you can drive to in twenty minutes, where one extra booked job obviously covers your fee. Then start. The lane sharpens with data you don't have yet.

Why most people get this wrong

Two mistakes, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is picking a niche you can't reach. Somebody reads that med spas have high margins, so they decide they serve med spas — nationally. Now they're a stranger on the internet competing with fifty other strangers on the internet, with no reason for anyone to pick them.

The second is refusing to pick at all. "I'll work with any local business." That sounds flexible and it's actually the most expensive choice on the board, because every conversation starts from scratch. You can't reuse a single sentence, and you never build the thing that makes client three easier than client one.

There's a third thing worth naming, and it contradicts most advice you'll hear.

You will probably not choose your niche. Your niche will choose you — whoever says yes first. That is a completely acceptable way to start, as long as you notice it happening.

Confirm before publication This section asserts that Isaiah's lane emerged rather than being chosen up front, based on "one client led to another." If he actually picked deliberately, this whole section should be cut, not softened — the value here is honesty, and a hedged version is worse than either true version.

The reason this matters: if you believe you must pick correctly before starting, then picking becomes a high-stakes decision and you'll avoid it. If you believe you're picking a direction that will sharpen once real businesses start replying, you'll pick this afternoon.

Both are true. The direction matters. The precision doesn't, yet.

The system

The six screens

Four of these come from Alex Hormozi's criteria for choosing an audience — a growing market, one with money, one that's easy to target, one in pain. They're good and I use them. The last two are mine, and for a beginner working a local market they matter more than the first four.

1. Growing. Are there more of these businesses this year than last? A shrinking market means you're fighting for a smaller pot every quarter.

2. Can afford you. What does one job pay them? A business where the average job is $180 cannot pay you what a business where the average job is $4,000 can, no matter how much they like you.

3. Easy to find. Can you build a list of 200 of them in an hour? If they're not on Google Maps, not in a directory, and not in a trade association, you'll spend your time on list-building instead of outreach.

4. In pain. Do they visibly need more customers? Competition is a good sign here, not a bad one. If nobody's advertising in a vertical, that's often because it doesn't work.

5. Can you reach the owner directly? This is the screen nobody talks about and it decides everything early on. In a two-person landscaping company, the owner answers the phone. In a regional chain, you'll spend four weeks getting past a gatekeeper to a marketing manager who has a vendor already. Early on, you want businesses small enough that the person who can say yes is the person who picks up.

6. Does one extra job obviously pay your fee? Do the arithmetic out loud, in their terms. If a roofer closes one in three estimates and a roof is $9,000 at 30% margin, then one extra estimate a month is roughly $900 in margin. If you're charging $500 a month, the math is not a debate — it's arithmetic they can do in their head while you're talking. If you have to build a spreadsheet to justify your fee, pick a different vertical.

The radius

Set it at twenty to thirty minutes of driving, then stop expanding.

Density beats size. Two hundred qualifying businesses inside twenty minutes is worth more than two thousand spread across the state, for four reasons that compound:

You can show up in person, which almost nobody does anymore. They know the streets you name. One client's referral is likely to be another business in the same radius. And your case study is about a business their customers actually use.

The instinct is to widen the radius when outreach is slow. Resist it. A slow week inside a tight radius is a message problem or a volume problem, and widening the map hides both.

Failure modes

You pick by margin alone.
High-margin verticals are usually the ones every agency already targets. Weight reachability and the fee arithmetic above margin — a slightly less profitable vertical where the owner picks up the phone will make you more money in year one.
You widen the radius when it gets quiet.
Hold the radius and fix the message instead. Widening the map converts a diagnosable problem into an undiagnosable one.
You pick something you can't get access to.
Before committing, spend twenty minutes trying to reach three owners in the vertical. If you can't get a human in three tries, that's your answer, and you found it in twenty minutes instead of two months.
You change lanes after four bad weeks.
Four bad weeks is not a verdict on a market, it's a verdict on 200 attempts. Apply the 300-attempt rule from Chapter 2 before you conclude anything about the vertical.
You pick a vertical with no seasonality awareness.
Landscaping in January and snow removal in July are different businesses. Check when your vertical's money actually moves, and plan outreach to arrive a month before their season starts, not during it.

The metric

What to track in this stage

Qualifying businesses inside your radius. One number, counted once. Open a map, search your vertical, count what's inside the boundary you drew.

If the answer is under 100, either the radius is too tight or the vertical is too narrow — widen one of them, once, and recount.

If the answer is over 500, you have plenty and the constraint is your daily number, not your market.

What "good" looks like: enough to sustain your daily number for six months without repeating yourself. At 20 attempts a day, five days a week, that's roughly 400 businesses with room for follow-ups. Do that arithmetic with your own number.

Do this now

45 minutes Open a map. Draw a twenty-minute radius around where you live.

Pick three service verticals inside it — the ones you'd guess first are fine. Score each one against the seven rows in the table above. Don't research; use what you already believe. The scorecard's job is to make you decide, not to be right.

Take the winner. Write one sentence on a note you'll see tomorrow:

"I sell to [vertical] within [radius]."

Then open the map again and count how many of them there are. That number is your market, and you now have one.

4 The Domino System · Stage 2 — Offer

Build the Offer They Say Yes To

By the end of this chapter you'll have one offer, at one price, described in one sentence you can say out loud without flinching.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words The first version of your offer and what was wrong with it. What you were selling before you figured out what to sell. The moment a business owner's face changed when you described it differently.

Here's a sentence I hear from beginners constantly, and it loses the deal before the deal starts:

"I do digital marketing for local businesses."

Nobody buys digital marketing. A landscaper doesn't wake up wanting digital marketing. He wakes up wanting his crew booked solid in April so he doesn't have to lay anybody off in May.

The gap between those two sentences is the entire chapter.

The principle

The principle

Sell the thing they can count. For a local service business, that thing is a booked appointment on their calendar — not leads, not traffic, not marketing.

Why most people get this wrong

Beginners sell inputs. Experienced operators sell outputs.

An input is something you do: run ads, build a funnel, manage social, send emails. An output is something the client gets: a plumber on a job site, an estimate on the calendar, a signed contract.

Selling inputs feels safer because it's what you actually control. If you promise "I'll run your ads," you can always say you ran the ads. But it puts you in a terrible position, because the client has no way to tell whether it worked, which means the only thing they can evaluate is the invoice.

There's a middle version that's almost as bad: selling leads. Leads sound like an output but they're not, because "lead" has no agreed definition. You think a lead is a form fill. He thinks a lead is somebody who wants to buy. You deliver forty and he says none of them were real, and you're both right, and now you're arguing.

A booked appointment is the smallest unit both of you will agree happened. That's why it's the thing to sell.

An appointment has a time on it. It's on a calendar. Either a person showed up or they didn't. There's nothing to argue about, which means there's nothing to argue about when the invoice comes either.

The system

1. Define your unit of sale precisely

Write it down, in the agreement, in the words you'd use with a lawyer standing there.

A weak definition: "qualified leads."

A strong definition: "a scheduled appointment with a decision-maker in your service area who has confirmed the time by text or phone."

The strong one names four things — that it's scheduled, who it's with, where they are, and what confirmation means. Every one of those is a fight you're not having in month three.

2. Make the value obvious before you say the price

This is where the Value Equation is useful. Alex Hormozi's framing is that an offer's value goes up with the size of the outcome and the buyer's belief it'll work, and down with how long it takes and how much hassle it costs them. It's a good lens. Here's what it looks like applied to a guy with a truck and three employees.

The outcome is not "more customers." It's "six more estimates on your calendar in the next thirty days." Numbers and a timeframe, in his units.

Belief it'll work is the hard one when you're starting out and have no case study. What you have instead is specificity. A person who can describe exactly what will happen — where the leads come from, what the follow-up looks like, what happens when someone doesn't answer — is more believable than a person with a vague promise and a testimonial from an unrelated industry.

Time to result is why Chapter 11 exists. If your setup takes three weeks because you didn't start messaging registration on day one, you've made your own offer worse.

Effort and sacrifice is where most local offers quietly fail. If your system requires the owner to answer his phone within five minutes during a workday, you've handed a roofer on a roof a job he can't do. Build the follow-up so it works when he's holding a nail gun.

3. Scope it hard

Write down what you don't do. Out loud, in the proposal.

Not because you're being difficult, but because an unbounded offer gets eaten. You'll agree to run appointment generation and four months later you're editing his website, designing a truck wrap, and answering texts about his Google review from a customer who's mad about a hedge.

Every one of those was you being helpful. Together they're the reason your effective hourly rate collapsed.

4. Get it to one sentence

You should be able to say the whole thing in a breath:

Price Against the Job, Not the Competition

By the end of this chapter you'll price from what one closed job is worth to your client, split your fee to match how value actually arrives, and know the signal that says you're too cheap.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words What you charged the first client. What you charge now. And the specific moment you raised your price — did you sweat it, what did you say, and what happened?

Almost every beginner prices the same way, and it's the worst of the three available methods.

They look at what other agencies charge, land somewhere slightly under it, and call that competitive.

Think about what that actually is. You've set your price using information about somebody else's business — their costs, their team, their market, their overhead — none of which you know and none of which applies to you. You've copied a number off a stranger's homework.

The principle

The principle

Price from what one closed job is worth to the client. Their arithmetic sets your ceiling. Your costs and your competitors are irrelevant to them.

Why most people get this wrong

There are three ways to set a price, and they're worth naming because most people use one by accident. This framing comes from Alex Hormozi's pricing work, and it's the clearest version I've seen.

Cost-plus is your costs plus a margin. It's simple and it's wrong here, because your client has no idea what it costs you and wouldn't care if he did. Your software bill is not his problem.

Competitor-based is the copying described above. It's the most common and the most damaging, because it caps you at whatever the least ambitious agency in your market decided to charge.

Value-based is pricing against what the outcome is worth to the buyer. It takes more thinking and it's the only one that scales.

Here's why the third one matters so much in local service. The gap between what an appointment costs you to produce and what it's worth to the client is enormous, and it's enormous in a way that's provable to him in about fifteen seconds.

Do the arithmetic in his units

Sit with a roofer. Ask two questions.

"What's an average job worth to you?" Say $9,000.

"Out of every three estimates you go out on, how many turn into work?" Say one.

So one estimate is worth $3,000 in revenue to him on average. At a 30% margin, roughly $900 in his pocket.

Now you say your price. If you're charging $1,500 a month and delivering six estimates, you've put roughly $5,400 of margin on his calendar for $1,500. That's not a negotiation. That's arithmetic he just did in his head while you were talking.

If you have to build a spreadsheet to justify your fee, your price is wrong or your vertical is wrong. The good version fits in one breath.

Notice what happened there. You never mentioned what other agencies charge. You never mentioned your costs. You anchored on his numbers, which he trusts, because he gave them to you.

The system

1. Split the fee to match how the value arrives

This is the highest-value idea in the chapter and almost nobody starting out does it.

You deliver two different kinds of value, and they arrive on completely different schedules.

One-time value: the build. Registration, the account, the pipeline, the automations, the calendar, the follow-up sequences. That work has a beginning and an end, and its value lands once, up front.

Ongoing value: running it. Managing spend, adjusting messaging, handling replies, reporting, fixing what breaks. That value arrives continuously in small amounts.

If you bundle both into one monthly number, two bad things happen. Your build gets underpriced, because a month of management doesn't feel like it should cost what three weeks of setup costs. And your retainer gets overpriced relative to what shows up each month, which makes you fragile.

So: a one-time setup fee for the build, and a smaller monthly fee for the management.

What you're charging for Shape Why
Registration, account build, automations, sequences One-time setup fee Value lands once. Paid before the work, which also filters out non-buyers
Management, optimization, replies, reporting Monthly retainer Value arrives continuously and should be judged continuously
Ad spend Paid by client, direct to platform Never run this through your account. It inflates your revenue, wrecks your margins, and makes you a bank

2. Understand the lookback window

Here's something that took me a while to see, and it explains most agency churn.

Clients don't judge you across the relationship. They judge you across the last billing cycle.

You can deliver a spectacular January. If March is quiet, the conversation in April is about March. The January result doesn't carry forward the way you think it should — in his head, January was paid for by January's invoice.

This has two consequences. Report every single month, even a bad one, so the window always has something in it. And bill on the longest cycle your client will accept, because a longer cycle gives you a longer window to be judged over.

3. Three tiers, named for what they get

Different clients have genuinely different budgets, and one price leaves money on both ends. But three tiers only work if the buyer can tell instantly which one is his.

Starter Standard Full
Setup $____ one-time $____ one-time $____ one-time
Monthly $____ $____ $____
Channels One Two Two + retargeting
Follow-up Automated Automated + manual Automated + manual + call
Reporting Monthly Weekly Weekly + call

4. Read the signals that say you're too cheap

Two of them, and both are counterintuitive because they feel like success.

You're closing more than half the people who hear your price. A high close rate feels great and usually means you're leaving money on the table. If nearly everyone says yes, the number isn't testing anything.

You're at capacity. If you can't take another client, price is the only lever you have left. Raising it is not greed at that point, it's arithmetic — you physically cannot sell more hours.

Raising prices does two things at once, which is why it's so powerful. You make more per client, and you serve fewer clients for the same revenue, which cuts your delivery cost. Both sides move in your favor.

The cost is that you'll hear no more often. That's the trade, and it's a good one — but it's an emotional cost, not a financial one, and people quietly reverse good price increases because hearing no is unpleasant.

Failure modes

You bundle setup into the monthly and underprice the build.
Split them. The build has a start and an end; charge for it once, up front, before the work begins.
You run client ad spend through your own card.
Have the client put their own card on the ad platform from day one. Fronting spend turns you into an unsecured lender to a small business, and you will eventually meet the client who doesn't pay.
You discount to close instead of adjusting scope.
If the price is too high, remove something — a channel, a reporting cadence, manual follow-up. Cutting price with scope unchanged teaches the client that your number was fiction.
You raise prices on new clients and never on existing ones.
Put a modest annual adjustment in the agreement from the start, so it's a term they agreed to rather than a conversation you have to nerve yourself up for.
You take a client who can't afford the outcome.
If one extra job doesn't obviously cover your fee, no price works. That's a lane problem, not a pricing problem — go back to Chapter 3.

The metric

What to track in this stage

Close rate at the current price. Track it per price point, not overall. When you raise a price, the number you care about is total revenue collected, not the percentage who said yes.

Average revenue per client, first 90 days. Setup plus three months. This is the number that tells you whether your pricing structure works, because it captures both halves of the split.

What "good" looks like: a close rate low enough that you're occasionally losing on price. If you never lose a deal on price, you have never found your ceiling — and you can't know what you're leaving behind.

Do this now

35 minutes Find one business in your chosen vertical, on Google, in your radius.

Work out their arithmetic from public information and reasonable estimates. What does a job cost? Search it. How many estimates does a business like that likely close? Ask anyone you know in the trade, or estimate one in three.

Write down: value of one appointment to this business = job value × close rate.

Now write your setup fee and your monthly next to it, and check whether the ratio is obvious. If a business owner would need convincing rather than a calculator, adjust one of the two numbers until the arithmetic speaks for itself.

Paper That Protects You

By the end of this chapter you'll know what every clause in your agreement is actually preventing, and why Massachusetts makes a careless proposal more expensive than most places.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words A scope dispute, a late payment, or a client who wanted more than what was agreed. What happened, what the agreement did or didn't say, and what you changed afterward.

Read this before the chapter

I am not a lawyer. Nothing in this chapter or in the templates in the asset vault is legal advice, and reading it doesn't create any kind of attorney-client relationship. Every template here is a starting point for a conversation with a licensed attorney in your own state — not a document to sign as-is. Laws differ by state and they change. Get it reviewed. It costs less than you think and enormously less than the thing it prevents.

Most people starting out work off a screenshot of a proposal they found in a Facebook group, or nothing at all.

Nothing at all is more common than you'd guess. A text message that says "sounds good, let's start Monday" is how a real percentage of local agency work begins.

That works fine right up until it doesn't, and when it doesn't, you find out that every ambiguity you left in gets resolved against you — because the other person's memory of what you agreed is genuinely different from yours, and neither of you is lying.

The principle

The principle

Every clause in your agreement exists because someone, somewhere, had that exact argument. You're not being formal. You're borrowing other people's scar tissue.

Why most people get this wrong

Two failures, opposite directions.

Too little paper. A handshake and an invoice. Feels fast and friendly. The problem isn't that your client is dishonest — most aren't. The problem is that "you'll handle my marketing" means different things to the two of you, and you won't discover the gap until month three when he asks why his website isn't done.

Too much paper, copied blind. Someone downloads a fourteen-page agency contract written for a firm with eleven employees and sends it to a guy with a truck. He doesn't read it, he feels talked down to, and half the clauses don't apply. Worse, you can't explain your own document, which is the real failure — if a client asks what a clause means and you don't know, you've lost more trust than the clause was protecting.

You should be able to explain every clause in your own agreement in one sentence, without notes. If you can't, take it out or learn it.

The system

Two documents, not one

The MSA — Master Services Agreement. The rules of the relationship. Signed once. Payment terms, liability, confidentiality, IP ownership, what happens when someone wants out. This doesn't change per client.

The SOW — Statement of Work. What you're actually doing, for how much, over what period. One per engagement. Short. This is the document that changes.

Splitting them means you can add a second engagement, or change scope, without reopening the whole relationship. You write a new SOW and the MSA keeps governing.

The clauses that matter, and what each one prevents

Clause What it prevents
Scope, stated positively and negatively The four-month drift where you're editing his website. List what's included and what isn't. The exclusions do more work than the inclusions
Definition of the deliverable The "these leads aren't real" argument. Define a booked appointment precisely — scheduled, with a decision-maker, in the service area, confirmed how
Client responsibilities Being blamed for a failure that was theirs. If they must respond to appointments within a set time, provide access, or supply their own card for ad spend — write it down

Massachusetts makes this sharper

If you're operating here, one statute deserves your attention: Massachusetts General Laws chapter 93A.

93A prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in trade or commerce. Most people know it as a consumer protection law — that's section 9. The one that matters to you is section 11, which covers business-to-business claims. Your client is a business. That's the section you live under.

What makes it sharp is the remedy. Where a violation was willful or knowing, or where a party refused to settle in bad faith after a written demand, a court can award up to treble damages plus the other side's attorney's fees.

Sit with that for a second. In a dispute over a $1,500-a-month engagement, the fees can dwarf the amount in dispute.

So the practical instruction is simple and it costs you nothing:

Describe the service. Never predict their income.

"Six booked estimates a month" is a service commitment you define and meet. "This will add $40,000 to your year" is a prediction about a business you don't control, and if it doesn't happen, you've handed someone a sentence to point at.

The same rule protects your clients

This isn't only about you. Your clients are going to run ads, collect reviews, and make claims to their own customers — and the FTC's Endorsement Guides, revised in July 2023, require testimonial claims to be truthful and to represent typical results, with material connections disclosed clearly and conspicuously. If you write ad copy that overstates, you've built your client's exposure. Chapter 17 covers this in full.

One session with a Massachusetts business attorney to review an MSA and SOW template you'll reuse for every client is among the best money you'll spend in year one. You're not buying a document — you're buying a reviewed template that serves fifty engagements.

Bring a draft. Attorneys charge less to fix something than to originate it.

You start work before anything is signed.
Signature and setup fee first, work second. No exceptions, including for the friend-of-a-friend — especially for the friend-of-a-friend.

The Daily Number

By the end of this chapter you'll run a repeatable outreach day inside a pipeline instead of a notebook, and know within a week whether it's working.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words What an actual day looked like while you were doing all three — class, lifts, practice, film, and the agency. Where the outreach physically happened. The time on the clock. What you gave up to fit it in.

Chapter 2 was about the commitment. This one is about the machinery, because a daily number you can't execute cleanly turns into a daily number you stop hitting.

The difference between someone who does 20 attempts a day for six months and someone who intends to is almost never discipline. It's friction. If sitting down to do outreach requires you to first decide who to contact, find their number, remember whether you already messaged them, and figure out what to say — you've built a job that takes an hour of setup before any work happens.

Nobody sustains that. So we remove it.

The principle

The principle

Build the list before you need it, and track every touch outside your head. Your memory of how much outreach you did is always wrong, in whichever direction is least useful.

Why most people get this wrong

They treat outreach as an activity instead of a system, and it shows up in three ways.

They build the list and do outreach in the same session. Two hours blocked, ninety minutes finding businesses, thirty minutes messaging. It feels like a productive afternoon. It's 30 minutes of work.

They track in their head or in a notes app. Which means they don't know who they've contacted, when, or how many times. So they either double-message people, which is embarrassing, or they never follow up, which is where most of the results live.

They quit on someone after one attempt. This is the big one. Most people stop after the first try. The reply you were going to get was often on attempt four.

The follow-up is not the annoying part of outreach. The follow-up is the outreach. The first message just starts the clock.

The system

1. Build the list on a different day than you work it

Batch it. One session a week — an hour, maybe two — where you do nothing but add businesses to the list.

For a local service vertical, your sources are boring and effective. Google Maps searched by town, one town at a time. Trade association directories. The town's business listing. Facebook groups for the area. Driving around with your phone, which sounds ridiculous and works, because a truck with a phone number on it is a lead nobody else in your market is collecting.

What you need per business: name, owner's name if you can get it, phone, email if available, one specific detail. That last field matters more than the rest — it's what makes the first line of your message not sound like a blast.

2. Six pipeline stages, no more

In GoHighLevel or whatever you use, build one pipeline for outreach with these stages:

Stage What it means What moves it forward
Cold On the list, never contacted First touch sent
Touched Contacted, no response yet Any reply, or exhausting the sequence
Replied Responded, including "not interested" A real back-and-forth
Conversation Engaged past the first exchange Call scheduled
Call Booked Time on the calendar Call happens
Proposal Out They've heard the price Signature or a no

Six stages you'll actually maintain beats twelve you'll abandon in a fortnight. And every one of these is countable, which means at any moment you can answer "where is this actually breaking?"

That question is the whole reason for the pipeline. A month from now you'll have a bad week, and the difference between "outreach isn't working" and "I have 40 in Replied and 2 in Call Booked, so my problem is the transition to a call" is the difference between panic and a fix.

3. Sequence the follow-up, don't improvise it

Decide in advance how many touches a business gets before you stop, and on what schedule. Write it once. Then it's not a decision you make while tired.

A reasonable shape for local service, spread across roughly two weeks: first touch, then a follow-up a day or two later, then a couple more spaced out across the following week, then a final one. Five to seven touches total, front-loaded.

Vary the channel. If you emailed first, the second touch can be a call. The third can be a different angle entirely rather than a nudge on the same one.

Then move them to a long-term list rather than deleting them. A landscaper who ignored you in February may be desperate in April. Nothing about your business changed — his season did.

Before any of this touches SMS

Text messaging to businesses through an application — which is what your CRM is — requires A2P 10DLC registration. Since February 2025, US carriers block unregistered traffic. Not throttle. Block. If SMS is in your sequence, read Chapter 11 and start registration before you build any of this, because it runs on carrier time, not yours.

4. Run the day the same way every time

Open the pipeline. Work the Touched column first — follow-ups before new contacts, always, because follow-ups convert better and they're the thing you'll skip if you leave them last.

Then new contacts from Cold until you hit your number.

Then log. Three tallies: attempts, replies, conversations.

Close it. Don't check it again at 11pm. The number is done or it isn't, and refreshing your inbox is not outreach.

Scripts That Don't Sound Like Scripts

By the end of this chapter you'll understand the anatomy underneath a message that gets answered, and you'll have written your own in four channels.

Before publication, replace each with Isaiah's actual message — the one that produced replies — and label it as his. A structure the reader fills in is honest and useful. I'm going to give you the anatomy before I give you anything to copy, because the anatomy is the part that transfers and the copy isn't.

Every message that ever gets answered is doing the same four things in the same order, whether it's a DM or a walk-in. Once you can see the four, you can write your own for any channel, and you'll stop needing scripts.

The principle

The principle

A cold message earns a reply by proving you looked. Everything else — the offer, the credentials, the pitch — is worthless until that's established in the first line.

Why most people get this wrong

Beginners write about themselves.

"Hi, my name is ___ and I run a digital marketing agency helping local businesses grow with proven strategies."

Read that as a landscaper with 400 unread emails. Nothing in it is about him. Nothing in it proves the sender knows he exists. It could have been sent to ten thousand people because it was.

The reason this happens is that you're nervous and credentials feel like armor. So you lead with who you are, because you're trying to establish that you're legitimate.

But legitimacy isn't what's blocking the reply. Being ignored is what's blocking the reply. And you don't beat being ignored with credentials — you beat it by being visibly not-a-blast.

The second mistake is asking for too much. A cold message that ends in "do you have 15 minutes for a call this week?" is asking a stranger to give up a chunk of their day before they know what this is. The first ask should be tiny.

The goal of the first message is not a call. It's a reply. Any reply. Once someone has written back once, you're in a conversation, and conversations are a completely different game than cold contact.

The system

The four-part anatomy

1. Proof you looked. One specific, checkable detail about their business. Not "I love your work." Something you could only know by looking — a truck you saw, the town they serve, a job on their feed, the fact they're booked out, the fact their website has no way to request a quote.

This is the whole first line and it does 80% of the work.

2. The observation. One sentence connecting that detail to a problem or an opportunity. Not a pitch. An observation. You're demonstrating that you understand their business, not selling yet.

3. What you do, in one line, in their units. Booked estimates. Booked jobs. Filled calendar. Not "marketing," not "lead generation," not "growth."

4. A tiny ask. A yes/no question that costs nothing to answer. "Worth a look?" "Want me to send it?" "Are you taking new work in April?"

That's it. Four moves. Two to four sentences total.

The Discovery Call

By the end of this chapter you'll run a call that diagnoses before it pitches and ends in a decision instead of "let me think about it."

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words A call that went badly. What you did wrong, what the owner said, and the specific thing you changed afterward. The worse it went, the better the chapter opens.

The most common way to lose a deal on a discovery call is to start selling in the first two minutes.

You've worked for weeks to get this call. The adrenaline is up. The prospect says "so what do you guys do?" — and you tell him. Fully. For four minutes. With enthusiasm.

And you've just lost, because you pitched a solution to a problem you haven't identified yet, in front of a man who now knows you're here to sell rather than to understand. Everything after that is him being polite.

The principle

The principle

Diagnose before you prescribe. A price quoted before you understand their problem is a number. A price quoted after is a solution.

Why most people get this wrong

Three reasons, and they're all understandable.

Nerves. Silence is uncomfortable and talking fills it. So you fill it with your own material.

You think the call is your audition. It isn't. The call is your diagnosis. He already decided you were worth thirty minutes — that part's done.

Nobody taught you what to ask. Most people can name what they sell but couldn't list ten questions that would tell them whether this person should buy. So they default to presenting.

Here's the reframe that fixes all three at once. You are not on this call to convince him. You are on it to find out whether this is going to work. Some of these people you should not take. A call run as a diagnosis lets you find that out; a call run as a pitch doesn't.

The person asking the questions is running the call. If he's asking and you're answering, you're not selling — you're being interviewed.

The system

Before: five minutes of homework

Look at the business. Their site, their reviews, their social, whether there's an obvious way for a customer to request a quote. Write down two things you noticed and one guess about their constraint.

That's it. Five minutes, so you walk in with something specific.

The call, in six moves

1. Frame it (30 seconds). Say how long it'll take, what you're going to do, and what happens at the end. And if you're recording, say so here.

Opening frame

"Thanks for making time. Quick heads up, I record these so I can go back through my notes — that alright with you? So here's how I run these. About twenty minutes. I'm going to ask you a bunch of questions about how you're getting work right now, because I don't know yet whether I can help you. At the end, one of two things happens. Either I think I can help and I'll tell you exactly what that looks like and what it costs — or I don't, and I'll tell you that too and point you somewhere better. Either way you'll know today. Sound good?"

That last part does a lot of work. You've told him a no is on the table, which means you're not desperate. And you've said he'll get an answer today, which quietly rules out "let me think about it."

The recording line is not optional in Massachusetts

M.G.L. c. 272 § 99 makes it a felony to secretly record a call — up to five years and a $10,000 fine. The operative word in the statute is "secretly." Announce it at the top and get an acknowledgment, and you're on the right side of it. Massachusetts law governs recordings made in-state regardless of where the other party is sitting, and the federal one-party-consent rule doesn't save you. If you'd rather not deal with it, don't record. But don't record quietly.

2. Current state. How work actually arrives today. Get specifics, not categories.

3. The gap. What they want that isn't happening. Quantify it.

4. What they've tried. This is the most valuable section and most people skip it. It tells you what objections are coming, what they've been burned by, and what words to avoid.

5. Cost of doing nothing. If this stays the same for another year, what does that cost? Let him say it. If you say it, it's a pitch; if he says it, it's a fact.

6. Prescribe, then price, then ask. Now — and only now — you tell him what you'd do, what it costs, and you ask for the decision.

Ask for the decision

Then stop talking. This is the hardest sentence in the chapter to actually do.

The close

"So based on what you told me — [restate his gap in his own words] — here's what I'd do. [The offer, thirty seconds.] That's [setup] to build it and [monthly] to run it. Do you want to do it?"

Then silence. Let it be uncomfortable. Whoever speaks first loses the point, and if you fill the gap you'll fill it with a discount.

Book the next thing before you hang up

If it's not a yes today, do not end on "I'll follow up." That's how deals evaporate.

Get the next meeting on the calendar while you're both live. This idea comes from Sharran Srivatsaa via Alex Hormozi's lead nurture work, and it's called booking a meeting from a meeting. Somebody being vague about the next step is an objection, not a scheduling problem — handle it like one.

Failure modes

You pitch in the first three minutes.
When he asks what you do, answer in one sentence and return a question. "I put booked estimates on contractors' calendars. Before I get into it — how are people finding you right now?"
You skip what they've tried before.
Ask it every time. A prospect burned by a previous agency has objections you can address before they surface, and you'll only know if you ask.
You quote a price and keep talking.
Say the number and stop. Practice the silence — it's a physical skill, and it's worth more than any script in this book.
You let the call end without a next date.
Open the calendar on the call. "I'll follow up" means it's over and neither of you has said so.
You never disqualify anybody.
If nobody fails your call, you aren't diagnosing. Taking a client whose arithmetic doesn't work costs you more than the revenue — it costs you the two months you spend failing to deliver.
You record without saying so.
Say the line. In Massachusetts this is a felony statute, not a formality, and it takes four seconds.

The metric

What to track in this stage

Show rate. Booked calls that actually happened. If this sags, the problem is in the gap between booking and the call, not in the call itself — reminders, timing, how far out you're letting people schedule.

Close rate. Of calls that happened, how many bought.

Track both, always. Close rate alone rewards cherry-picking — a rep who only takes easy calls looks excellent and produces less. The number that matters is calls booked to deals closed, end to end.

What "good" looks like: improving against your own last twenty. And per Chapter 5, a close rate above half usually means your price is low, not that your calls are great.

An objection is not resistance. It's information, arriving late.

Every objection you hear on a call is a question the prospect had at minute four and didn't ask. By the time it comes out of his mouth as "let me think about it," it's been sitting there getting heavier for twenty minutes.

Which means the best objection handling happens before the objection. Most of this chapter is about hearing them early.

The principle

The principle

Every objection is one of three things: they don't believe it'll work, they don't believe it'll work for them, or the timing is genuinely wrong. Find out which before you answer.

Why most people get this wrong

They treat objections as things to overcome, so they argue. And the moment you argue, you've turned a conversation into a contest, and the prospect's job becomes defending his position instead of considering yours.

There's also a habit of answering the words instead of the meaning. "It's too expensive" gets a discount. But "too expensive" almost never means "I don't have the money" — it usually means "I don't yet believe this returns more than it costs," which a discount doesn't fix. It makes it worse, because you've just proved the number was made up.

Answer what they meant, not what they said. Discounting a belief problem just tells them your price was never real.

The system

Three moves, in order

1. Acknowledge it as reasonable. Not "I understand, but." Just agree that it's a fair thing to be thinking.

2. Ask one question to find out which of the three it is. This is the move nobody makes. You cannot answer an objection you haven't diagnosed, and the same eight words can mean three different things.

3. Answer the actual version. Short. Then return to the decision.

1. "It's too expensive." Not a budget problem — a return problem. He can't yet see the arithmetic Go back to his own numbers. Job value × close rate = what one appointment is worth. Let the arithmetic answer it, not you

5. "I get all my work from referrals." Usually true, and usually flat. Referrals don't scale on purpose Ask what happens in a slow month. Referral flow is a number he doesn't control, and most owners have felt that

On number three

Objection three deserves special attention because it's the one your buyer profile is built around. A large share of local owners have already paid someone and gotten nothing.

The instinct is to distance yourself: "yeah, a lot of agencies are bad." Don't. It sounds like every other salesperson and it teaches him nothing.

Ask instead. What did they do? How long? What did the reporting look like? Did anyone ever tell you what was actually happening?

Two things come out of that. You learn the specific failure, which lets you address the specific thing. And he gets to say it out loud to someone who's listening, which is worth more than any rebuttal you had ready.

What works is being the most specific person in the conversation. Age reads as inexperience only in the absence of precision. Someone who can describe exactly what will happen in week one, week two, and week three doesn't sound young. He sounds like he's done it.

Register Before You Build

By the end of this chapter you'll have messaging registration started on day one instead of discovering it in week three — and you'll know why that single sequencing decision protects the client relationship.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words Your own A2P 10DLC registration. What got rejected and why. How long it actually took versus what you expected. What you'd tell someone doing it Monday morning.

This is the least exciting chapter in the book and probably the most valuable one.

Nobody makes content about carrier registration. There's no thumbnail in it. But it is the single most common reason a new agency's first client spends three weeks paying for silence — and a client who pays for three weeks of silence is a client who leaves in month two.

Accurate as of August 2026

Carrier requirements, fees, and processing times in this chapter change. Everything below reflects the rules and pricing in effect as of August 2026. Verify current requirements with your provider before you rely on any specific number here. The sequencing advice — register first — does not change.

The principle

The principle

Registration runs on carrier time, not your time. It is the only part of the build you cannot compress, so it starts on day one — before the pipeline, before the automations, before anything.

Why most people get this wrong

They build in the order that feels natural, which is the order of visible progress.

Set up the account. Build the pipeline. Write the automations. Design the follow-up sequence. Test it — and discover the texts aren't going anywhere. Then start registration, which takes another week or two, during which the client is watching nothing happen and wondering what he's paying for.

The failure isn't technical. Everyone gets registered eventually. The failure is sequencing, and it costs you the most valuable asset you have with a new client: the first thirty days, when their patience is at its maximum and their doubt is at its minimum.

Your client's goodwill is highest on day one and it depletes daily. Spending the first three weeks of it on a form you could have filed on day one is the most avoidable mistake in this business.

What A2P 10DLC actually is

Application-to-Person messaging over 10-digit long codes. In plain terms: when software sends a text from a normal-looking phone number, that's A2P. It's what your CRM does.

US carriers — AT&T, T-Mobile, Verizon — built a registration system to cut down spam. You register who you are and what you're going to send, and they decide whether your traffic gets through.

The part people miss: since February 2025, unregistered A2P traffic is blocked outright. Not slowed. Not delivered to spam. Blocked. Your messages leave your system, report as sent, and never arrive. You can look at a dashboard full of sent messages while the client's leads hear nothing.

Two things register, and they're separate:

The brand is the business. Legal name, EIN, address, contact. One per business.

The campaign is the use case. What kind of messages, sample copy, and how people opted in. One brand can have several campaigns.

The system

1. Collect what you need before you open the form

Half of rejections come from mismatched details. The legal name and EIN have to match IRS records exactly — not the trade name, not the name on the truck.

  • Legal business name, exactly as registered with the IRS
  • EIN, and the address on the EIN filing
  • Business type and industry
  • Website URL, live, with a visible privacy policy
  • Contact name, email on the business domain, and phone
  • Sample messages — the actual text you'll send
  • A description of how people opt in, plus a screenshot of the form

2. Fix the website first

The most common rejection cause is a website that doesn't back up the application.

The reviewer is checking that you are what you say you are. So the site needs to be live, to describe the business the campaign describes, and — this is the one people miss — to have a privacy policy that specifically mentions SMS, stating that mobile information isn't sold or shared with third parties for marketing.

If your client's opt-in is a web form, that form needs visible consent language next to the submit button. Not buried in terms. Next to the button.

3. Register the brand, then the campaign

Brand first. It's usually approved in one to three business days.

Then the campaign, which is the slower and pickier half: three to seven business days, and ten to fifteen during busy periods.

Campaign rejections almost always trace to one of three things: sample messages that don't match the stated use case, opt-in language that doesn't show real consent, or a use case description too vague to evaluate.

Write sample messages that are actually representative. If you'll send appointment reminders, the sample should be an appointment reminder with the placeholders filled in — not a generic greeting.

The First Booked Appointment

By the end of this chapter you'll know which single moment predicts whether a client stays — and you'll have built your entire onboarding to reach it faster.

There's a moment in every client relationship where the thing stops being a promise and becomes a fact.

The principle

The principle

A lead is a promise. An appointment is a fact. Your client can't tell the difference between a promise and a lie until one of them turns into a fact.

Two things are running down at the same time in month one.

The client's patience, which is at its highest on day one and drops a little every day nothing visible happens.

By the end of this chapter you'll report in your client's language, on a rhythm that protects you, and you'll know what to say in a month that went badly.

Impressions. Reach. Click-through rate. Cost per click. Engagement rate. Twelve charts.

The principle

The principle

Report the numbers your client already thinks in. For a local service business that's appointments booked, appointments shown, and jobs won. Everything else is your diagnostic, not his report.

Why most people get this wrong

Two reasons, and the second is the uncomfortable one.

They report what the platform gives them. The ad dashboard produces impressions and CTR, so that's what goes in the report. But those are your inputs, not his outputs. He never asked for impressions. He asked for a full calendar.

They report volume when results are thin. This is the honest reason. When a month is bad, twelve charts of activity are a way of demonstrating effort without stating the outcome. Every client eventually learns to read a fat report as a hiding place.

A one-page report that leads with a bad number builds more trust than a twelve-page report that buries it. He already knows the month was slow. He's watching to see whether you'll say so.

The lookback window

This is the mechanic that explains most agency churn, and once you see it you can't unsee it.

Clients do not judge you across the relationship. They judge you across the last billing cycle.

You deliver an outstanding January. February is decent. March is slow. In April, the conversation is entirely about March — and if you say "but look at January," he hears an excuse. In his head, January was already paid for by January's invoice.

The framing comes from Alex Hormozi's pricing work, and it has three practical consequences.

Report every cycle, without exception. A month with no report is a month where the window is empty and he fills it with whatever he's feeling.

Bill on the longest cycle he'll accept. A longer billing period is a longer window to be judged over, which smooths out the variance that's inherent in this work.

Front-load the value. A result in week one of a cycle sits in the window the whole time. A result in the last week barely registers before the invoice lands.

"Jobs won (you told us)"

You cannot verify what your client closed. You know what you delivered; you don't know what happened at the kitchen table. So label it as reported rather than measured.

How Not to Get Fired in Month Two

By the end of this chapter you'll spot a client leaving weeks before they say anything, and you'll know why adding more deliverables usually makes it worse.

Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words The client you lost. What the last month looked like, what they said when they cancelled, and what actually broke — which is usually not what they said.

Nobody cancels on the day they decide to cancel.

They decide weeks earlier, usually in a quiet moment, and then they wait until the next invoice makes it convenient. By the time you get the message, the decision is old. Whatever you say in reply is arriving at a conversation that already finished without you.

Which means the entire skill here is noticing early.

The principle

The principle

When a client leaves, the reflex is to go find another one. But churn is a delivery problem, and prospecting harder to cover it is running to stand still.

Why most people get this wrong

The arithmetic is brutal and most people never do it.

Say you're adding two clients a month and losing one. You feel busy — you're doing outreach, you're closing, there's activity everywhere. Net, you're growing by one a month. Now cut the loss to zero and you're growing by two, on identical effort.

The second version isn't twice as good. It's better than that, because the clients who stay refer, and the ones who leave sometimes talk. Retention compounds in both directions.

Two clients in, one out feels like a busy month. It's half a month's growth for a full month's work.

And there's a cost nobody puts in the spreadsheet. A client who leaves unhappy in a market of 200 businesses inside a twenty-minute radius is not a neutral event. That's the same radius your referrals come from.

Adding more is usually the wrong move

Here's the counterintuitive part.

When a client goes quiet, the instinct is to give them more. More reporting. An extra channel. A new deliverable thrown in free. It feels generous and it's usually the wrong response.

Alex Hormozi makes this point in his retention work and it matches what happens in the field: overwhelm is a leading cause of churn. Adding things to a relationship that's already unclear makes it less clear. The client didn't want more. He wanted the one thing he bought to visibly work.

The right response to a quiet client is almost always to make the existing result more visible, not to add a new thing to be confused about.

Tip the Next Domino

Everything up to here gets you one client and keeps them. This is where one client becomes two, and where the arithmetic of the whole business changes.

Because here's what a referred prospect skips. He skips the cold message. He skips the part where you prove you're a real person. He skips most of "have you done this before." He arrives having already been told by someone he trusts that you're worth the call.

Same offer. Same you. A fraction of the work.

The principle

The principle

Ask at the moment the result lands, not at renewal. Gratitude has a half-life, and it is much shorter than you think.

Why most people get this wrong

They wait, and they wait for the wrong signal.

The instinct is to wait until you've "earned it" — three months in, a solid track record, the relationship established. By which point the specific thing that made him happy is a general feeling that things are going fine, and general feelings don't produce introductions.

The moment to ask is the moment something specific and good happens. He tells you he closed a $9,000 job that came off an appointment you booked. That day. Not next quarter.

A referral is not a favor you've earned over time. It's a reflex somebody has in a good moment. Be there for the moment.

The second mistake is asking for a referral rather than for a name.

"Do you know anyone who might need this?" is a question that requires the other person to do a database search of everyone they know while standing in a parking lot. Almost everyone says "let me think about it," which means no.

The system

1. Ask on the day, not on the schedule

Build the trigger into your process. When a client reports a job won — which he does because your monthly report asks him, per Chapter 14 — that's the cue.

Two things happen in the same conversation. You acknowledge the specific result. Then you ask.

2. Ask for a category, not for "anyone"

The fix for the parking-lot problem is to narrow the search for him.

Instead of "do you know anyone," you name the group. "Who else do you know doing landscaping around Norwood?" or "Who do you use for [an adjacent trade]?"

That's a question with an answer. He can picture two or three faces immediately.

Adjacent trades are the underrated version. A landscaper knows a tree guy, a mason, a fence installer, a pool company. None of them compete with him, all of them have the same problem, and he has no reason to hesitate.

The referral ask

"That's the [Nth] job off this — nice. Quick thing while I've got you. Who else do you know doing [ADJACENT TRADE] around here? Not asking you to sell anything - just whether there's someone I should introduce myself to." [He names someone.] "Perfect. Easiest thing: text him my name and say I did X for you. I'll take it from there. Want me to send you something you can just forward?"

That last line does the real work. Most referrals die because the client agrees and then never does it — not from reluctance, but because writing the message is a small task that never reaches the top of the list.

So you write it for him.

3. Send the forwardable message

What you send him to forward

"Hey [NAME] - the guy who handles our booked estimates is [YOUR NAME]. He set up the system that's been filling our calendar since [MONTH]. Local, over in [TOWN]. Worth a conversation. His number is [NUMBER]."

Short enough to forward without editing. Written in his voice, not yours. And it names the specific thing rather than describing you as a marketing person.

4. Capture the testimonial in the same moment

The best time to get a testimonial is the same moment as the referral ask, for the same reason.

Ask for something specific rather than general praise. "Would you mind saying what the calendar looked like before, and what it looks like now?" produces something usable. "Would you write a testimonial?" produces three sentences of adjectives.

Get it as a voice note if you can. Voice notes are easier for people to give and far more convincing than text.

Get permission in writing, and be accurate

Before you use a client's name, logo, numbers, or words in your own marketing, get written permission naming exactly what you may use and where. An email reply saying yes is fine — keep it.

Two rules on top of that. Under the FTC's Endorsement Guides, revised effective July 2023, testimonial claims must be truthful and represent typical results, and material connections must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously — so if you paid, discounted, or gave anything in exchange for a testimonial, that has to be disclosed. And if you describe a client's outcome, don't imply it's what a new client should expect unless it genuinely is. In Massachusetts, an overstated claim also runs into c. 93A, which carries treble damages.

Describe what happened. Don't promise it will happen again.

5. Where the referral enters your pipeline

Back at VOLUME, from Chapter 7 — but on a short path.

A referral does not get the cold sequence. It gets a first message that names the person who sent you, and a direct ask for a call. The whole four-part cold anatomy from Chapter 8 collapses, because the proof-you-looked step has already been done by someone he trusts.

Track referred leads separately in your pipeline. Their close rate will be different, and knowing the gap tells you exactly what referral flow is worth — which tells you how much delivery quality is worth, since that's what produces it.

6. When you finally narrow the lane

Once you have enough clients, stage seven loops back to stage one.

Look at the ones who stayed longest and paid most. Find what they have in common — vertical, size, how they found you, what they needed. That common thread is the lane you should have picked, and now you know it from data instead of guessing.

Then narrow deliberately. This is the moment where the niche stops being an accident and starts being a choice, and it's a moment you can only reach by having done the work first.

Failure modes

You wait for the right time to ask.
Ask the day a specific good thing happens. There is no better moment coming, and the one you're in is already fading.
You ask "do you know anyone?"
Name a category or an adjacent trade. Give him a search he can run in his head in two seconds.

Staying Legal

By the end of this chapter you'll know the four rule sets that govern this work, what each one requires of you, and which one is scheduled to change.

Read this first

I am not an attorney. Nothing in this chapter is legal advice and reading it creates no attorney-client relationship. This is a working summary written so you know what exists and what to ask about — not a substitute for a licensed attorney in your own state reviewing your actual practices.

Everything here is accurate as of August 2026. Two of these four areas have changed within the last eighteen months and one has a further change scheduled for January 2027. Verify current requirements before you rely on anything in this chapter.

Most people running small agencies have no idea how much regulated activity they're doing.

You're sending text messages to people you don't know. You're recording phone calls. You're making claims about business outcomes in writing. You're publishing testimonials. Every one of those has a rule set attached, and two of them carry criminal or multiplied-damages exposure.

The good news is that compliance here is mostly a handful of specific behaviors, not a legal education. This chapter is that handful.

The principle

The principle

Four rule sets govern this work: how you message, how you record, what you promise, and how you use proof. Learn what each one requires once, build it into your process, and stop thinking about it.

1. TCPA — how you message

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act governs calls and texts. It's the one with the most active litigation, and the ground has moved twice recently.

What changed, and when

Date What happened
Jan 2025 The Eleventh Circuit vacated the FCC's "one-to-one consent" rule in Insurance Marketing Coalition v. FCC, holding the FCC had gone beyond its authority in redefining prior express written consent. The rule had been days from taking effect
Apr 2025 The revocation rule took effect — opt-out requests must be honored, and honored promptly
Aug 2025 The FCC reinstated the prior standard for prior express written consent following the vacatur
Jan 2027 Scheduled: the broader "revocation-all" requirement — under which one opt-out would revoke consent across a sender's messaging purposes and channels — takes effect January 31, 2027, having been delayed from 2026

That last row is inside this book's shelf life. If you're reading this in 2027, that change has landed. Check where it stands before you build anything that depends on treating opt-outs channel-by-channel.

What to actually do

Get real consent and keep the evidence. For marketing messages you need prior express written consent. In practice: a checkbox that isn't pre-ticked, consent language visible next to the submit button, and a stored record of what the form said, when they submitted, and from what IP.

Honor opt-outs immediately and broadly. Don't be clever about the scope. Someone who says stop, stops — across everything you're sending them. Given what's scheduled for 2027, building this the broad way now means you don't rebuild later.

Registration is not consent. Chapter 11 gets your messages delivered by the carriers. It has nothing to do with whether you had permission to send them. Two different systems, two different exposures.

Interrogate every list a client hands you. This is the one that catches agencies. A client gives you 4,000 contacts and says "these are all customers." Ask how they were collected, when, and what they agreed to. Put responsibility for consent in the agreement, per Chapter 6 — and still ask, because a contract clause doesn't undo a message you already sent.

2. Massachusetts recording law — how you record

If you record sales calls, and Chapter 9 suggests you might, this applies to you.

It's a secrecy statute, not a consent statute

Massachusetts is commonly called a "two-party consent" state. That shorthand is close enough for conversation and slightly wrong in a way that matters.

M.G.L. c. 272 § 99 defines "interception" as to secretly hear or record a wire or oral communication. Secretly is the operative word in every offense under the statute.

The practical effect is straightforward and easier than the shorthand suggests: if everyone knows the recording is happening, there is no violation. An announcement at the top of the call — including an automated one — puts the parties on notice.

What to actually do

Announce it before the conversation starts. The opening frame in Chapter 9 has the line built in. Get an acknowledgment.

Understand the interstate rule. Massachusetts law governs recordings made in-state regardless of where the other party is located. The federal one-party-consent baseline does not override it.

Take it seriously. This is a felony statute — up to five years in state prison and a $10,000 fine, plus civil damages. Almost nothing else in this book carries criminal exposure. This does, and avoiding it costs you one sentence at the start of a call.

If you don't want to deal with it, don't record. That's a legitimate choice. What isn't legitimate is recording quietly.

3. Chapter 93A — what you promise

Covered in Chapter 6, restated here because it touches everything.

M.G.L. c. 93A prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in trade or commerce. Section 9 covers consumers; section 11 covers business-to-business claims. Your client is a business, so section 11 is your exposure.

The remedy is what makes it sharp. Where a violation was willful or knowing, or where a party refused to settle in bad faith after a written demand, a court can award up to treble damages plus the other side's attorney's fees. In a dispute over a modest monthly engagement, the fees can exceed the amount in dispute several times over.

What to actually do

Describe the service, never predict their income. "Six booked estimates a month" is a commitment you define and control. "This will add $40,000 to your year" is a prediction about someone else's business.

Audit every document you send. Proposal, pitch deck, email templates, the note in your phone, your website. Search for guarantee, will make, ROI, and promise.

Deliver what the agreement says. The cleanest 93A defense is a clear scope you met.

4. FTC Endorsement Guides — how you use proof

Revised effective July 26, 2023 (16 CFR Part 255). This governs testimonials and endorsements — both yours and the ones you create for clients.

What it requires

Material connections must be disclosed. Paid, discounted, gifted, employed, related — if there's a connection between you and the endorser that a consumer wouldn't expect, disclose it.

Claims must be truthful and represent typical results. A best-case outcome presented as representative is deceptive.

"Clear and conspicuous" has teeth. The standard is that a disclosure be difficult to miss and easily understood by ordinary consumers — unavoidable, standing out by color, size, placement, and duration. Fine print at the bottom does not meet it.

The definition of endorsement is broad. It covers reviews, tags, social mentions, virtual influencers, fake reviews, and organizational seals — not just formal testimonials.

What to actually do

Get written permission before using any client's name, numbers, or words. Keep the reply.

Label results honestly. If a result is exceptional, say so.

Check the ad copy you write for clients. This is the part agencies miss. If you write a claim on a client's behalf that overstates what their customers typically get, you built their exposure. Review your own creative against this rule, because they're not going to.
