<h1>The Daily Number</h1>
<p class="chap-promise">By the end of this chapter you'll run a repeatable outreach day inside a pipeline instead of a notebook, and know within a week whether it's working.</p>

<div class="storyslot"><b>Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words</b>
What an actual day looked like while you were doing all three — class, lifts, practice, film, and the agency. Where the outreach physically happened. The time on the clock. What you gave up to fit it in.</div>

Chapter 2 was about the commitment. This one is about the machinery, because a daily number you can't execute cleanly turns into a daily number you stop hitting.

The difference between someone who does 20 attempts a day for six months and someone who intends to is almost never discipline. It's friction. If sitting down to do outreach requires you to first decide who to contact, find their number, remember whether you already messaged them, and figure out what to say — you've built a job that takes an hour of setup before any work happens.

Nobody sustains that. So we remove it.

## The principle

<div class="principle"><span class="label">The principle</span><p>Build the list before you need it, and track every touch outside your head. Your memory of how much outreach you did is always wrong, in whichever direction is least useful.</p></div>

## Why most people get this wrong

They treat outreach as an activity instead of a system, and it shows up in three ways.

**They build the list and do outreach in the same session.** Two hours blocked, ninety minutes finding businesses, thirty minutes messaging. It feels like a productive afternoon. It's 30 minutes of work.

**They track in their head or in a notes app.** Which means they don't know who they've contacted, when, or how many times. So they either double-message people, which is embarrassing, or they never follow up, which is where most of the results live.

**They quit on someone after one attempt.** This is the big one. Most people stop after the first try. The reply you were going to get was often on attempt four.

<p class="pull">The follow-up is not the annoying part of outreach. The follow-up is the outreach. The first message just starts the clock.</p>

## The system

### 1. Build the list on a different day than you work it

Batch it. One session a week — an hour, maybe two — where you do nothing but add businesses to the list.

For a local service vertical, your sources are boring and effective. Google Maps searched by town, one town at a time. Trade association directories. The town's business listing. Facebook groups for the area. Driving around with your phone, which sounds ridiculous and works, because a truck with a phone number on it is a lead nobody else in your market is collecting.

What you need per business: name, owner's name if you can get it, phone, email if available, one specific detail. That last field matters more than the rest — it's what makes the first line of your message not sound like a blast.

### 2. Six pipeline stages, no more

In GoHighLevel or whatever you use, build one pipeline for outreach with these stages:

<table>
<thead><tr><th>Stage</th><th>What it means</th><th>What moves it forward</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><strong>Cold</strong></td><td>On the list, never contacted</td><td>First touch sent</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Touched</strong></td><td>Contacted, no response yet</td><td>Any reply, or exhausting the sequence</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Replied</strong></td><td>Responded, including "not interested"</td><td>A real back-and-forth</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Conversation</strong></td><td>Engaged past the first exchange</td><td>Call scheduled</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Call Booked</strong></td><td>Time on the calendar</td><td>Call happens</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Proposal Out</strong></td><td>They've heard the price</td><td>Signature or a no</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

Six stages you'll actually maintain beats twelve you'll abandon in a fortnight. And every one of these is countable, which means at any moment you can answer "where is this actually breaking?"

That question is the whole reason for the pipeline. A month from now you'll have a bad week, and the difference between "outreach isn't working" and "I have 40 in Replied and 2 in Call Booked, so my problem is the transition to a call" is the difference between panic and a fix.

### 3. Sequence the follow-up, don't improvise it

Decide in advance how many touches a business gets before you stop, and on what schedule. Write it once. Then it's not a decision you make while tired.

A reasonable shape for local service, spread across roughly two weeks: first touch, then a follow-up a day or two later, then a couple more spaced out across the following week, then a final one. Five to seven touches total, front-loaded.

Vary the channel. If you emailed first, the second touch can be a call. The third can be a different angle entirely rather than a nudge on the same one.

Then move them to a long-term list rather than deleting them. A landscaper who ignored you in February may be desperate in April. Nothing about your business changed — his season did.

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
### Before any of this touches SMS
Text messaging to businesses through an application — which is what your CRM is — requires A2P 10DLC registration. Since February 2025, US carriers block unregistered traffic. Not throttle. Block. If SMS is in your sequence, read Chapter 11 and start registration before you build any of this, because it runs on carrier time, not yours.
</div>

### 4. Run the day the same way every time

Open the pipeline. Work the Touched column first — follow-ups before new contacts, always, because follow-ups convert better and they're the thing you'll skip if you leave them last.

Then new contacts from Cold until you hit your number.

Then log. Three tallies: attempts, replies, conversations.

Close it. Don't check it again at 11pm. The number is done or it isn't, and refreshing your inbox is not outreach.

## The asset

<p class="script-label">The outreach day — a repeatable block</p>
<div class="script">BEFORE THE BLOCK (once a week, separate session)
  [ ] Add 100+ businesses to Cold
  [ ] Each row: name, owner, phone, email, one specific detail

THE BLOCK (same time daily)
  [ ] Work Touched: every follow-up due today
  [ ] Work Cold: new contacts until the number is hit
  [ ] Move anyone who replied into Replied
  [ ] Log: attempts ___  replies ___  conversations ___
  [ ] Close it

WEEKLY (4 minutes, Sunday)
  [ ] Total the three columns
  [ ] Reply rate = replies / attempts
  [ ] Count what is sitting in each pipeline stage
  [ ] Name the single biggest drop-off. That is next week's problem.</div>

## Failure modes

<dl class="failure" markdown="1">
<dt>List-building eats the outreach block.</dt>
<dd>Separate sessions on separate days. If you're finding businesses during the outreach block, you're not doing outreach.</dd>
<dt>You do first touches and skip follow-ups.</dt>
<dd>Work the Touched column before the Cold column, every single day. New contacts are more fun and less productive.</dd>
<dt>Your pipeline has fourteen stages and nobody updates it.</dt>
<dd>Cut back to six. A pipeline that's out of date is worse than no pipeline, because you'll trust it and it'll be wrong.</dd>
<dt>You delete the no-replies.</dt>
<dd>Move them to a long-term list with a date to revisit. Seasonality does more of your selling than your message does.</dd>
<dt>Everything piles up in one stage and you don't notice.</dt>
<dd>Count the stages weekly. Forty in Replied and two in Call Booked is a specific, fixable problem — but only if you look.</dd>
</dl>

## The metric

<div class="metric" markdown="1">
### What to track in this stage
**Stage-to-stage conversion.** Cold to Touched, Touched to Replied, Replied to Conversation, Conversation to Call Booked, Call Booked to Proposal Out.

This is the real diagnostic in the whole book. Every stage-to-stage number points at a different fix. Weak Touched-to-Replied means your message or targeting. Strong replies but weak conversations means your opener is interesting and your offer isn't. Plenty of conversations but few calls booked means you're not asking for the call directly enough.

**What "good" looks like:** your own numbers from last month. Nothing else. Published benchmarks come from other people's lists in other people's markets, and the only honest comparison is against yourself.
</div>

<div class="donow" markdown="1">
### Do this now
<span class="time">45 minutes</span>
Build the pipeline. Six stages, exactly as named above, in whatever CRM you're using. If you don't have one yet, a spreadsheet with a Stage column is completely adequate for your first hundred.

Then spend the remaining time adding businesses to Cold from the vertical and radius you picked in Chapter 3. Name, owner, phone, and one specific detail per row.

Target 40 rows. If you get 25, that's fine — you have a list, which is more than you had this morning, and tomorrow's block now has somewhere to start.
</div>
