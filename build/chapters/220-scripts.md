I'm going to give you the anatomy before I give you anything to copy, because the anatomy is the part that transfers and the copy isn't.

Every message that ever gets answered is doing the same four things in the same order, whether it's a DM or a walk-in. Once you can see the four, you can write your own for any channel, and you'll stop needing scripts.

## The principle

<div class="principle"><span class="label">The principle</span><p>A cold message earns a reply by proving you looked. Everything else — the offer, the credentials, the pitch — is worthless until that's established in the first line.</p></div>

## Why most people get this wrong

Beginners write about themselves.

*"Hi, my name is ___ and I run a digital marketing agency helping local businesses grow with proven strategies."*

Read that as a landscaper with 400 unread emails. Nothing in it is about him. Nothing in it proves the sender knows he exists. It could have been sent to ten thousand people because it was.

The reason this happens is that you're nervous and credentials feel like armor. So you lead with who you are, because you're trying to establish that you're legitimate.

But legitimacy isn't what's blocking the reply. **Being ignored is what's blocking the reply.** And you don't beat being ignored with credentials — you beat it by being visibly not-a-blast.

The second mistake is asking for too much. A cold message that ends in "do you have 15 minutes for a call this week?" is asking a stranger to give up a chunk of their day before they know what this is. The first ask should be tiny.

<p class="pull">The goal of the first message is not a call. It's a reply. Any reply. Once someone has written back once, you're in a conversation, and conversations are a completely different game than cold contact.</p>

## The system

### The four-part anatomy

**1. Proof you looked.** One specific, checkable detail about their business. Not "I love your work." Something you could only know by looking — a truck you saw, the town they serve, a job on their feed, the fact they're booked out, the fact their website has no way to request a quote.

This is the whole first line and it does 80% of the work.

**2. The observation.** One sentence connecting that detail to a problem or an opportunity. Not a pitch. An observation. You're demonstrating that you understand their business, not selling yet.

**3. What you do, in one line, in their units.** Booked estimates. Booked jobs. Filled calendar. Not "marketing," not "lead generation," not "growth."

**4. A tiny ask.** A yes/no question that costs nothing to answer. "Worth a look?" "Want me to send it?" "Are you taking new work in April?"

That's it. Four moves. Two to four sentences total.

The anatomy stays. The length and tone shift.

### On the walk-in

I want to make a case for this, because it's the channel most people skip and it's the one where a young local operator has an actual advantage.

An agency three time zones away cannot walk into a landscaping office in your town on a Tuesday morning. You can. You know the streets. You can name the intersection. And the owner is a real person who is far more likely to give thirty seconds of attention to someone standing in front of him than to a name in his spam folder.

It's uncomfortable. That's most of why it works — the discomfort is exactly what keeps the channel uncrowded.

Go at a slow hour for their trade. Ask for the owner by name, which requires having looked him up, which is itself the proof-you-looked step. Say the four-part message. Leave one page and get out. Three minutes.

<div class="script">[SPECIFIC DETAIL — something only visible by looking at their

[ONE-LINE OBSERVATION connecting that detail to a problem or a
window of opportunity. No pitch.]

[WHAT YOU DO, in their units — booked estimates, filled calendar.]

[TINY ASK — a yes/no question.]</div>

<p class="script-label">Email — structure</p>
<div class="script">subject: [lowercase, specific to them, no pitch]

[SPECIFIC DETAIL.]

[OBSERVATION — one or two sentences.]

[WHAT YOU DO, one line, in their units.]

[TINY ASK.]

[First name]</div>

<p class="script-label">Cold call — opener structure</p>
<div class="script">"Hey [NAME], this is [YOUR NAME] — I'm local, over in [TOWN].
This is a cold call, so I'll be quick.

[SPECIFIC DETAIL + OBSERVATION, one breath.]

[WHAT YOU DO, one line.]

Is that something you'd want to hear about, or should I
leave you alone?"</div>

Naming the cold call as a cold call is deliberate. It disarms the thing they're already thinking, and the explicit exit at the end gets you a real answer rather than a polite dodge — which is worth more, because a fast no is a cheap no.

**SMS requires registration.** Cold texting through a CRM without A2P 10DLC registration means your messages are blocked outright, not delivered slowly. Chapter 11.

**Recorded calls in Massachusetts.** If you record calls, M.G.L. c. 272 § 99 applies to any recording made in-state. The statute turns on secrecy — announce it at the top and you're compliant. It's a felony if you don't. Chapter 17 covers it, and Chapter 9 puts the announcement in the script where it belongs.

## Failure modes

<dl class="failure" markdown="1">
<dt>Your first line is about you.</dt>
<dd>Delete the first sentence of every message you've written and see whether it still works. It usually does, and it's usually better.</dd>
<dt>The "specific detail" is generic praise.</dt>
<dd>"Love your work" is not a detail. If the sentence could be sent to any business in the vertical, it isn't proof you looked and it does nothing.</dd>
<dt>You ask for a call in the first message.</dt>
<dd>Ask a yes/no question instead. Get the reply first; the call is what you ask for after they've written back once.</dd>
<dt>You personalize so heavily you can only send six a day.</dt>
<dd>One specific detail is enough. Research beyond that is procrastination wearing a productive costume — the number still has to get hit.</dd>
<dt>You judge a script on twenty sends.</dt>
<dd>Three hundred, per Chapter 2. Twenty sends tells you nothing except how you felt about sending twenty.</dd>
</dl>

## The metric

<div class="metric" markdown="1">
### What to track in this stage
**Reply rate by channel.** Track them separately — a message that works in a DM often dies in email, and averaging them hides which channel is carrying you.

**Positive reply rate.** Of the people who wrote back, how many were open rather than telling you to go away? A high reply rate made mostly of annoyance means your targeting is off, not your writing.

**What "good" looks like:** better than your last 300. Then better than that.
</div>

<div class="donow" markdown="1">
### Do this now
<span class="time">40 minutes</span>
Pick five businesses off the Cold list you built in Chapter 7.

For each one, spend three minutes finding a single specific, checkable detail. Write it in the row.

Then write five messages — one per business — following the four-part anatomy. Not five copies of one message. Five messages that share a skeleton and differ in the first line.

Read each one out loud before you send it. If it sounds like something you'd say to a person, send it. If it sounds like something a company would say, rewrite the first line and read it again.

Then send all five.
