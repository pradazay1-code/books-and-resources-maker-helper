Most people starting out work off a screenshot of a proposal they found in a Facebook group, or nothing at all.

Nothing at all is more common than you'd guess. A text message that says "sounds good, let's start Monday" is how a real percentage of local agency work begins.

That works fine right up until it doesn't, and when it doesn't, you find out that every ambiguity you left in gets resolved against you — because the other person's memory of what you agreed is genuinely different from yours, and neither of you is lying.

## The principle

<div class="principle"><span class="label">The principle</span><p>Every clause in your agreement exists because someone, somewhere, had that exact argument. You're not being formal. You're borrowing other people's scar tissue.</p></div>

## Why most people get this wrong

Two failures, opposite directions.

**Too little paper.** A handshake and an invoice. Feels fast and friendly. The problem isn't that your client is dishonest — most aren't. The problem is that "you'll handle my marketing" means different things to the two of you, and you won't discover the gap until month three when he asks why his website isn't done.

**Too much paper, copied blind.** Someone downloads a fourteen-page agency contract written for a firm with eleven employees and sends it to a guy with a truck. He doesn't read it, he feels talked down to, and half the clauses don't apply. Worse, you can't explain your own document, which is the real failure — if a client asks what a clause means and you don't know, you've lost more trust than the clause was protecting.

<p class="pull">You should be able to explain every clause in your own agreement in one sentence, without notes. If you can't, take it out or learn it.</p>

## The system

### Two documents, not one

**The MSA — Master Services Agreement.** The rules of the relationship. Signed once. Payment terms, liability, confidentiality, IP ownership, what happens when someone wants out. This doesn't change per client.

**The SOW — Statement of Work.** What you're actually doing, for how much, over what period. One per engagement. Short. This is the document that changes.

Splitting them means you can add a second engagement, or change scope, without reopening the whole relationship. You write a new SOW and the MSA keeps governing.

### Massachusetts makes this sharper

If you're operating here, one statute deserves your attention: **Massachusetts General Laws chapter 93A.**

93A prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in trade or commerce. Most people know it as a consumer protection law — that's section 9. The one that matters to you is **section 11, which covers business-to-business claims.** Your client is a business. That's the section you live under.

What makes it sharp is the remedy. Where a violation was willful or knowing, or where a party refused to settle in bad faith after a written demand, a court can award **up to treble damages plus the other side's attorney's fees.**

Sit with that for a second. In a dispute over a $1,500-a-month engagement, the fees can dwarf the amount in dispute.

So the practical instruction is simple and it costs you nothing:

**Describe the service. Never predict their income.**

"Six booked estimates a month" is a service commitment you define and meet. "This will add $40,000 to your year" is a prediction about a business you don't control, and if it doesn't happen, you've handed someone a sentence to point at.

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
### The same rule protects your clients
This isn't only about you. Your clients are going to run ads, collect reviews, and make claims to their own customers — and the FTC's Endorsement Guides, revised in July 2023, require testimonial claims to be truthful and to represent typical results, with material connections disclosed clearly and conspicuously. If you write ad copy that overstates, you've built your client's exposure. Chapter 17 covers this in full.
</div>

### Get it reviewed once

One session with a Massachusetts business attorney to review an MSA and SOW template you'll reuse for every client is among the best money you'll spend in year one. You're not buying a document — you're buying a reviewed template that serves fifty engagements.

Bring a draft. Attorneys charge less to fix something than to originate it.

## Failure modes

<dl class="failure" markdown="1">
<dt>You start work before anything is signed.</dt>
<dd>Signature and setup fee first, work second. No exceptions, including for the friend-of-a-friend — especially for the friend-of-a-friend.</dd>
<dt>Your agreement doesn't say who owns the accounts.</dt>
<dd>Add the ownership clause today and apply it to existing clients at renewal. This is the fight that ends relationships badly, and it's completely avoidable.</dd>
<dt>A results promise sneaks into the proposal.</dt>
<dd>Search every document you send for the words guarantee, will make, and ROI. Rewrite each one as a description of the service you'll perform.</dd>
<dt>You use a template you can't explain.</dt>
<dd>Read your own agreement start to finish and write a one-sentence explanation beside each clause. Anything you can't explain gets removed or understood — there is no third option.</dd>
<dt>You message a list the client handed you without asking where it came from.</dt>
<dd>Put consent responsibility in writing, and ask how the list was collected before you send anything. Their list becomes your problem the moment you press send.</dd>
</dl>

## The metric

<div class="metric" markdown="1">
### What to track in this stage
**Percentage of active clients under a current signed agreement.** The target is 100%, and if it isn't, you already know which client you're thinking about.

**Days from proposal sent to signature.** A number that climbs usually means the proposal is doing too much explaining, not that clients got slower.

**What "good" looks like:** every client on paper, every paper reviewed once by an attorney, and no document in circulation containing a sentence about how much money the client will make.
</div>

<div class="donow" markdown="1">
### Do this now
<span class="time">30 minutes</span>
Open every document you currently send a prospect — proposal, email template, pitch deck, the note in your phone.

Search for four things: **guarantee**, **will make**, **ROI**, and **promise**.

Rewrite every hit as a description of what you'll do rather than what they'll earn. "We'll deliver six booked estimates a month" instead of "you'll add $40k a year."

Then open a calendar and book thirty minutes with a business attorney in your state for the MSA and SOW review. Booking it is the task. Do that part today, because it's the one you'll otherwise put off for a year.
</div>
