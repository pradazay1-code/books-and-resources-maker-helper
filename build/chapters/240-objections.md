An objection is not resistance. It's information, arriving late.

Every objection you hear on a call is a question the prospect had at minute four and didn't ask. By the time it comes out of his mouth as "let me think about it," it's been sitting there getting heavier for twenty minutes.

Which means the best objection handling happens before the objection. Most of this chapter is about hearing them early.

## The principle

<div class="principle"><span class="label">The principle</span><p>Every objection is one of three things: they don't believe it'll work, they don't believe it'll work for them, or the timing is genuinely wrong. Find out which before you answer.</p></div>

## Why most people get this wrong

They treat objections as things to overcome, so they argue. And the moment you argue, you've turned a conversation into a contest, and the prospect's job becomes defending his position instead of considering yours.

There's also a habit of answering the words instead of the meaning. "It's too expensive" gets a discount. But "too expensive" almost never means "I don't have the money" — it usually means "I don't yet believe this returns more than it costs," which a discount doesn't fix. It makes it worse, because you've just proved the number was made up.

<p class="pull">Answer what they meant, not what they said. Discounting a belief problem just tells them your price was never real.</p>

## The system

### Three moves, in order

**1. Acknowledge it as reasonable.** Not "I understand, but." Just agree that it's a fair thing to be thinking.

**2. Ask one question to find out which of the three it is.** This is the move nobody makes. You cannot answer an objection you haven't diagnosed, and the same eight words can mean three different things.

**3. Answer the actual version.** Short. Then return to the decision.

### On number three

Objection three deserves special attention because it's the one your buyer profile is built around. A large share of local owners have already paid someone and gotten nothing.

The instinct is to distance yourself: "yeah, a lot of agencies are bad." Don't. It sounds like every other salesperson and it teaches him nothing.

Ask instead. What did they do? How long? What did the reporting look like? Did anyone ever tell you what was actually happening?

Two things come out of that. You learn the specific failure, which lets you address the specific thing. And he gets to say it out loud to someone who's listening, which is worth more than any rebuttal you had ready.

### On number twelve

You will be the youngest person in most rooms you sell in. Some of the people you're pitching have been running their business longer than you've been alive.

The two failure modes are apologizing for it and overcompensating with jargon. Both read as insecurity.

What works is being the most specific person in the conversation. Age reads as inexperience only in the absence of precision. Someone who can describe exactly what will happen in week one, week two, and week three doesn't sound young. He sounds like he's done it.

## Failure modes

<dl class="failure" markdown="1">
<dt>You answer before you diagnose.</dt>
<dd>Ask one question first, every time. "Too expensive" has three different meanings and only one of them is about money.</dd>
<dt>You discount to beat a price objection.</dt>
<dd>Adjust scope instead — drop a channel or a reporting cadence. Cutting price with scope unchanged teaches him your number was invented.</dd>
<dt>You attack the previous agency.</dt>
<dd>Ask what happened and listen. Criticizing the last guy makes you sound like the last guy, who also criticized someone.</dd>
<dt>You accept "let me think about it" at face value.</dt>
<dd>Ask what specifically he wants to think through. Nine times in ten there's a real objection sitting underneath, and it's answerable.</dd>
<dt>You promise results to end the discomfort.</dt>
<dd>Have your service-level language written down before the call so you never improvise it. This is the objection most likely to make you say something you can't stand behind.</dd>
<dt>You treat every objection as winnable.</dt>
<dd>Some are real. A genuinely slammed contractor in peak season is not a close, he's a follow-up in eight weeks. Book that and move on — pushing costs you the future deal.</dd>
</dl>

## The metric

<div class="metric" markdown="1">
### What to track in this stage
**Objection frequency.** After every call, write down which objections came up. After twenty calls you'll have a ranked list, and it will not be the list you expected.

That ranking is worth more than this chapter. Whatever sits at the top is something to fix upstream — in your outreach, your qualifying, or your framing — rather than something to keep handling on calls.

**Close rate after objection.** Of calls where a given objection appeared, how many closed anyway? This tells you which of your answers are working and which just feel like they are.

**What "good" looks like:** the same objection appearing less often over time, because you started answering it before it was asked.
</div>

<div class="donow" markdown="1">
### Do this now
<span class="time">45 minutes</span>
Take the twelve above. For each one, write your own answer in three sentences or fewer, in the words you'd actually use — not the words in this book.

Then do the part that matters: **say each one out loud.** Any answer you can't say smoothly is one you won't say under pressure. Rewrite those until they come out clean.

Keep the sheet next to you on calls. Add a tally mark every time an objection comes up. In a month, that tally tells you exactly what to fix.
</div>
