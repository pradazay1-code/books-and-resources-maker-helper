<h1>How Not to Get Fired in Month Two</h1>
<p class="chap-promise">By the end of this chapter you'll spot a client leaving weeks before they say anything, and you'll know why adding more deliverables usually makes it worse.</p>
</div>

<div class="storyslot"><b>Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words</b>
The client you lost. What the last month looked like, what they said when they cancelled, and what actually broke — which is usually not what they said.</div>

Nobody cancels on the day they decide to cancel.

They decide weeks earlier, usually in a quiet moment, and then they wait until the next invoice makes it convenient. By the time you get the message, the decision is old. Whatever you say in reply is arriving at a conversation that already finished without you.

Which means the entire skill here is noticing early.

## The principle

<div class="principle"><span class="label">The principle</span><p>When a client leaves, the reflex is to go find another one. But churn is a delivery problem, and prospecting harder to cover it is running to stand still.</p></div>

## Why most people get this wrong

The arithmetic is brutal and most people never do it.

Say you're adding two clients a month and losing one. You feel busy — you're doing outreach, you're closing, there's activity everywhere. Net, you're growing by one a month. Now cut the loss to zero and you're growing by two, on identical effort.

The second version isn't twice as good. It's better than that, because the clients who stay refer, and the ones who leave sometimes talk. Retention compounds in both directions.

<p class="pull">Two clients in, one out feels like a busy month. It's half a month's growth for a full month's work.</p>

And there's a cost nobody puts in the spreadsheet. A client who leaves unhappy in a market of 200 businesses inside a twenty-minute radius is not a neutral event. That's the same radius your referrals come from.

### Adding more is usually the wrong move

Here's the counterintuitive part.

When a client goes quiet, the instinct is to give them more. More reporting. An extra channel. A new deliverable thrown in free. It feels generous and it's usually the wrong response.

Alex Hormozi makes this point in his retention work and it matches what happens in the field: **overwhelm is a leading cause of churn.** Adding things to a relationship that's already unclear makes it less clear. The client didn't want more. He wanted the one thing he bought to visibly work.

The right response to a quiet client is almost always to make the existing result more visible, not to add a new thing to be confused about.

**Usage churn is the one to internalize.** A client who has stopped logging in has already left; the billing just hasn't caught up. If you intercept at that point you can often save it. If you wait for the invoice, you can't.

### The check-in that prevents the cancellation

Every two to three weeks, one short message. Not a report — a conversation.

There's a simple structure for this that works because it's how people actually talk. Acknowledge something specific you've seen them do. Say something genuine about it. Then ask one question.

<p class="script-label">The check-in</p>
<div class="script">"Saw you closed the [SPECIFIC JOB] from the appointment
that came in Tuesday.

That's the third one from [SOURCE] this month - that
channel's working for you.

Quick question: are those the kind of jobs you want more
of, or would you rather I push toward [OTHER TYPE]?"</div>

Three things happen. He's reminded you're paying attention. He's reminded the thing is working, in a specific instance rather than a summary. And you get information that makes next month better.

Two minutes. Every two to three weeks. It does more for retention than any feature you could build.

One caution worth knowing: the bigger the client, the less hand-holding they usually want. Read the response rate and adjust — a client who replies in three words doesn't want a longer message, he wants a shorter one.

### The save conversation

Sometimes it gets to the cancellation anyway. You can save a real portion of these, but only on the phone.

**Get them on a call.** Text and email cancellations are almost impossible to reverse, because there's no room to hear the actual problem.

**Let them say the whole thing.** Don't defend anything until they've finished. The complaint you interrupt is not the real one.

**Get more annoyed about it than they are.** If the problem is legitimate, say so plainly. Only one person gets to be in the angry seat, and if you take it, they can't.

**Then find the specific unmet expectation.** Usually there's one. "So if the appointments had been showing up at the rate we talked about, you'd have stayed?" Now you know whether it's fixable.

**Offer one of two things, not five.** Either a redo — a defined window to fix the specific thing, with a date — or a change of fit, which sometimes means a smaller scope and sometimes means letting them go cleanly.

Letting one go cleanly is a real outcome and it's underrated. A client who leaves feeling handled says something different in the hardware store than one who leaves feeling ignored.

### Tell them about the exit conversation up front

At onboarding, say this: *"If you ever want to stop, I just ask for fifteen minutes first — either I fix it or I learn something."*

It costs nothing at signing and it converts a cancellation email into a conversation later.

## Failure modes

<dl class="failure" markdown="1">
<dt>You respond to a quiet client by adding deliverables.</dt>
<dd>Make the existing result more visible instead. Adding things to an unclear relationship makes it more confusing, not more valuable.</dd>
<dt>You only talk to clients when you're reporting.</dt>
<dd>Add the two-minute check-in every two to three weeks. It is the cheapest retention work available and almost nobody does it.</dd>
<dt>You don't notice the dashboard hasn't been opened in three weeks.</dt>
<dd>Check engagement weekly. Usage churn precedes actual churn by a month and it's visible the whole time.</dd>
<dt>You try to save a cancellation over email.</dt>
<dd>Get on the phone. If they won't, ask the one question that matters — what specifically would have had to be different.</dd>
<dt>You blame the client's follow-up without evidence.</dt>
<dd>Show the data. "Nineteen booked, six shown — let's look at what happens between the booking and the visit" is a conversation. "You're not following up" is an accusation.</dd>
<dt>You take the cancellation personally and go quiet.</dt>
<dd>Run the exit conversation anyway. The information is worth more than the discomfort, and the way you leave is what gets repeated locally.</dd>
</dl>

## The metric

<div class="metric" markdown="1">
### What to track in this stage
**Monthly churn.** Clients lost this month divided by clients you started the month with. Count only the opening cohort — new clients signed during the month don't change it.

**Average client lifetime, in months.** Roughly one divided by your monthly churn rate. This is the number that tells you what a client is actually worth, which tells you what you can afford to spend acquiring one.

**Reason for every loss.** Write it down every time, in the client's words and then in yours. After five, the pattern is usually one thing and it's usually fixable.

**What "good" looks like:** longer than last quarter. And it's worth knowing that serving very small businesses carries some structural churn — some of them will simply go out of business, and that isn't a reflection on your work. Track it separately from the losses you caused.
</div>

<div class="donow" markdown="1">
### Do this now
<span class="time">25 minutes</span>
If you have clients: open each account and check the last time they logged in or replied. Anyone past two weeks gets the check-in message today.

If you don't have clients yet: write your check-in template and put a recurring task in your calendar for every second Tuesday, starting the week you sign your first one.

Then add one line to your onboarding checklist: *"Tell them I ask for fifteen minutes before anyone cancels."* Say it on the kickoff call, when it's easy, so it exists later when it isn't.
