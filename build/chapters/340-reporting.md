<h1>Reporting That Keeps You Hired</h1>
<p class="chap-promise">By the end of this chapter you'll report in your client's language, on a rhythm that protects you, and you'll know what to say in a month that went badly.</p>
</div>

<div class="storyslot"><b>Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words</b>
A client who didn't believe the results were real. What he said, what you showed him, and how it resolved. Or the report you sent that made everything worse.</div>

Most agency reporting is written to look impressive rather than to be understood, and you can tell because of what's in it.

Impressions. Reach. Click-through rate. Cost per click. Engagement rate. Twelve charts.

Here's what the guy receiving it is thinking: *I don't know what any of this means, I don't know if it's good, and I'm paying $1,500 a month.*

He won't say that. He'll say "great, thanks." And then in month four he'll cancel, and you'll be surprised, because the reports were so thorough.

## The principle

<div class="principle"><span class="label">The principle</span><p>Report the numbers your client already thinks in. For a local service business that's appointments booked, appointments shown, and jobs won. Everything else is your diagnostic, not his report.</p></div>

## Why most people get this wrong

Two reasons, and the second is the uncomfortable one.

**They report what the platform gives them.** The ad dashboard produces impressions and CTR, so that's what goes in the report. But those are your inputs, not his outputs. He never asked for impressions. He asked for a full calendar.

**They report volume when results are thin.** This is the honest reason. When a month is bad, twelve charts of activity are a way of demonstrating effort without stating the outcome. Every client eventually learns to read a fat report as a hiding place.

<p class="pull">A one-page report that leads with a bad number builds more trust than a twelve-page report that buries it. He already knows the month was slow. He's watching to see whether you'll say so.</p>

## The lookback window

This is the mechanic that explains most agency churn, and once you see it you can't unsee it.

**Clients do not judge you across the relationship. They judge you across the last billing cycle.**

You deliver an outstanding January. February is decent. March is slow. In April, the conversation is entirely about March — and if you say "but look at January," he hears an excuse. In his head, January was already paid for by January's invoice.

The framing comes from Alex Hormozi's pricing work, and it has three practical consequences.

**Report every cycle, without exception.** A month with no report is a month where the window is empty and he fills it with whatever he's feeling.

**Bill on the longest cycle he'll accept.** A longer billing period is a longer window to be judged over, which smooths out the variance that's inherent in this work.

**Front-load the value.** A result in week one of a cycle sits in the window the whole time. A result in the last week barely registers before the invoice lands.

## The system

### One page, four numbers, in this order

<div class="script">THIS MONTH                    Aug 1 - Aug 31

  Appointments booked            ____
  Appointments shown             ____     (___% show rate)
  Jobs won (you told us)         ____
  Estimated revenue from those   $____

  Cost of the program            $____
  Cost per booked appointment    $____
  Cost per shown appointment     $____

WHAT CHANGED THIS MONTH
  ____________________________________________________

WHAT I AM CHANGING NEXT MONTH
  ____________________________________________________

WHAT I NEED FROM YOU
  ____________________________________________________</div>

Four numbers, then three sentences. That's the whole report and it fits on a phone screen.

The last three lines do more than the numbers. They tell him you're paying attention, that you have a plan, and — critically — that some of the outcome depends on him.

### "Jobs won (you told us)"

That parenthetical matters more than it looks.

You cannot verify what your client closed. You know what you delivered; you don't know what happened at the kitchen table. So label it as reported rather than measured.

Two things come of that. You're accurate, which protects you if anyone ever scrutinizes your claims. And you've created a monthly reason for him to tell you what closed — which is the number you need for your own case studies later, and the one most agencies never collect.

### Keep your diagnostics separate

You still need the platform numbers. Cost per click, creative performance, which source produces which appointments — that's how you improve.

Keep it in your own view, not his. If he asks, show him. If he doesn't, don't put it in front of him, because every number you add dilutes the four that matter.

### The weekly rhythm

Monthly reports alone leave too much silence.

**Weekly:** a two-line message. Appointments booked this week, anything that needs him. Thirty seconds to write, and it keeps the lookback window continuously full.

**Monthly:** the one-pager above.

**Quarterly:** a real conversation about what's working, what he wants more of, and whether the scope still fits. Chapter 15 covers what that call is actually for.

### The bad month

You will have them. The messaging goes stale, the season turns, a competitor starts spending, or it's just variance.

Send the report anyway, on time, with the bad number at the top.

<p class="script-label">The bad month message</p>
<div class="script">"August was down - 6 booked against 11 in July.

Here's what I think happened: [specific diagnosis, not
'the algorithm changed'].

Here's what I'm changing: [one or two specific things,
with dates].

Here's what I need from you: [something concrete, or
'nothing, this one's on me'].

I'll have the first read on whether it's working by
[date]."</div>

Three rules. Say the number first. Never blame the platform in the abstract — "the algorithm changed" is what people say when they haven't looked. And commit to a date when you'll know something, because the worst part of a bad month for a client isn't the bad month, it's not knowing whether anyone's on it.

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
### On results claims
The FTC's Endorsement Guides, revised effective July 2023, require that testimonial and endorsement claims be truthful and represent typical results, with material connections disclosed clearly and conspicuously — where "clear and conspicuous" means genuinely hard to miss, not buried in fine print.

This applies twice over. It governs how you use a client's results in your own marketing, and it governs the ad copy you write on their behalf. If you build an ad claiming an outcome that isn't typical for their customers, you've created their exposure, and in Massachusetts a deceptive claim also runs into c. 93A. Ask for written permission before using any client's numbers, and label what's typical. Chapter 17 has the detail.
</div>

## Failure modes

<dl class="failure" markdown="1">
<dt>Your report leads with impressions.</dt>
<dd>Delete every platform metric from the client-facing page. Keep them in your own view where they belong.</dd>
<dt>You skip the report in a bad month.</dt>
<dd>Send it on time with the bad number first. A missing report is read as hiding, and it's the moment a client starts looking around.</dd>
<dt>You report leads instead of appointments.</dt>
<dd>Report booked and shown. "Lead" is a word most local owners have already learned to distrust.</dd>
<dt>You never ask what closed.</dt>
<dd>Ask every month and label it as reported. It's the number that makes your case studies real, and nobody will volunteer it.</dd>
<dt>The report takes you two hours to produce.</dt>
<dd>Build the view once so the numbers are already there. A report that's expensive to make is a report you'll start skipping.</dd>
<dt>You use a client's results publicly without asking.</dt>
<dd>Get written permission naming exactly what you may share. Do it while they're happy, in writing, and keep it.</dd>
</dl>

## The metric

<div class="metric" markdown="1">
### What to track in this stage
**Cost per shown appointment.** The cleanest measure of whether the program works. It combines everything upstream into one number both of you can act on.

**Reporting consistency.** Percentage of months where the report went out on time. Target is 100%, and the months you miss will be exactly the bad ones — which is precisely when it matters.

**Client-reported jobs won.** Chase it monthly. It's the bridge between what you delivered and what he actually cares about, and it's the raw material for every case study you'll ever write.

**What "good" looks like:** a cost per shown appointment that's a clear fraction of what one job is worth to him, and a client who can state that ratio from memory. When he can say it himself, you're not being evaluated any more.
</div>

<div class="donow" markdown="1">
### Do this now
<span class="time">35 minutes</span>
Build the one-page report as a template — the four numbers and the three sentences, nothing else.

Then go into your platform and build the view that produces those four numbers without you assembling anything by hand. If it takes more than five minutes a month to fill in, simplify until it doesn't.

If you have a client already, send this month's version today, even mid-month, even if the numbers are ordinary. Getting the rhythm started matters more than the timing of the first one.
</div>
