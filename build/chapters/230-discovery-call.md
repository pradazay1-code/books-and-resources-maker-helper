<h1>The Discovery Call</h1>
<p class="chap-promise">By the end of this chapter you'll run a call that diagnoses before it pitches and ends in a decision instead of "let me think about it."</p>
</div>

<div class="storyslot"><b>Story needed — cold open, 150–300 words</b>
A call that went badly. What you did wrong, what the owner said, and the specific thing you changed afterward. The worse it went, the better the chapter opens.</div>

The most common way to lose a deal on a discovery call is to start selling in the first two minutes.

You've worked for weeks to get this call. The adrenaline is up. The prospect says "so what do you guys do?" — and you tell him. Fully. For four minutes. With enthusiasm.

And you've just lost, because you pitched a solution to a problem you haven't identified yet, in front of a man who now knows you're here to sell rather than to understand. Everything after that is him being polite.

## The principle

<div class="principle"><span class="label">The principle</span><p>Diagnose before you prescribe. A price quoted before you understand their problem is a number. A price quoted after is a solution.</p></div>

## Why most people get this wrong

Three reasons, and they're all understandable.

**Nerves.** Silence is uncomfortable and talking fills it. So you fill it with your own material.

**You think the call is your audition.** It isn't. The call is your diagnosis. He already decided you were worth thirty minutes — that part's done.

**Nobody taught you what to ask.** Most people can name what they sell but couldn't list ten questions that would tell them whether this person should buy. So they default to presenting.

Here's the reframe that fixes all three at once. **You are not on this call to convince him. You are on it to find out whether this is going to work.** Some of these people you should not take. A call run as a diagnosis lets you find that out; a call run as a pitch doesn't.

<p class="pull">The person asking the questions is running the call. If he's asking and you're answering, you're not selling — you're being interviewed.</p>

## The system

### Before: five minutes of homework

Look at the business. Their site, their reviews, their social, whether there's an obvious way for a customer to request a quote. Write down two things you noticed and one guess about their constraint.

That's it. Five minutes, so you walk in with something specific.

### The call, in six moves

**1. Frame it (30 seconds).** Say how long it'll take, what you're going to do, and what happens at the end. And if you're recording, say so here.

<p class="script-label">Opening frame</p>
<div class="script">"Thanks for making time. Quick heads up, I record these so I
can go back through my notes — that alright with you?

So here's how I run these. About twenty minutes. I'm going to
ask you a bunch of questions about how you're getting work
right now, because I don't know yet whether I can help you.

At the end, one of two things happens. Either I think I can
help and I'll tell you exactly what that looks like and what
it costs — or I don't, and I'll tell you that too and point
you somewhere better. Either way you'll know today.

Sound good?"</div>

That last part does a lot of work. You've told him a no is on the table, which means you're not desperate. And you've said he'll get an answer today, which quietly rules out "let me think about it."

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
### The recording line is not optional in Massachusetts
M.G.L. c. 272 § 99 makes it a felony to *secretly* record a call — up to five years and a $10,000 fine. The operative word in the statute is "secretly." Announce it at the top and get an acknowledgment, and you're on the right side of it. Massachusetts law governs recordings made in-state regardless of where the other party is sitting, and the federal one-party-consent rule doesn't save you. If you'd rather not deal with it, don't record. But don't record quietly.
</div>

**2. Current state.** How work actually arrives today. Get specifics, not categories.

**3. The gap.** What they want that isn't happening. Quantify it.

**4. What they've tried.** This is the most valuable section and most people skip it. It tells you what objections are coming, what they've been burned by, and what words to avoid.

**5. Cost of doing nothing.** If this stays the same for another year, what does that cost? Let him say it. If you say it, it's a pitch; if he says it, it's a fact.

**6. Prescribe, then price, then ask.** Now — and only now — you tell him what you'd do, what it costs, and you ask for the decision.

### The question bank

<p class="script-label">Discovery questions — ask in this order</p>
<div class="script">CURRENT STATE
  How do people find you right now?
  Where did your last five jobs come from?
  How many estimates do you go out on in a normal week?
  Out of ten estimates, how many turn into work?
  What's an average job worth to you?
  Who answers the phone when someone calls?
  What happens if someone fills out your form at 8pm?

THE GAP
  How many more jobs a week could you actually handle?
  What's stopping you from being there now?
  What does your calendar look like eight weeks out?
  When's your busy season, and when does the phone go quiet?

WHAT THEY'VE TRIED
  Have you worked with a marketing company before?
  What happened?
  What did you like about it? What made you stop?
  What are you doing yourself right now to get work?
  What's the last thing you tried that didn't work?

COST OF DOING NOTHING
  If it's the same twelve months from now, what does that
    cost you?
  Have you had to turn work down? Or lay anyone off?
  What would three more jobs a month change for you?

DECISION
  If we did this, who else is in on the decision?
  What would need to be true for you to say yes today?</div>

The last two are not throwaway. "Who else is in on the decision" asked at minute fifteen saves you the call where a spouse or a partner appears out of nowhere at the end.

### Ask for the decision

Then stop talking. This is the hardest sentence in the chapter to actually do.

<p class="script-label">The close</p>
<div class="script">"So based on what you told me — [restate his gap in his own
words] — here's what I'd do. [The offer, thirty seconds.]

That's [setup] to build it and [monthly] to run it.

Do you want to do it?"</div>

Then silence. Let it be uncomfortable. Whoever speaks first loses the point, and if you fill the gap you'll fill it with a discount.

### Book the next thing before you hang up

If it's not a yes today, do not end on "I'll follow up." That's how deals evaporate.

Get the next meeting on the calendar while you're both live. This idea comes from Sharran Srivatsaa via Alex Hormozi's lead nurture work, and it's called booking a meeting from a meeting. Somebody being vague about the next step is an objection, not a scheduling problem — handle it like one.

## Failure modes

<dl class="failure" markdown="1">
<dt>You pitch in the first three minutes.</dt>
<dd>When he asks what you do, answer in one sentence and return a question. "I put booked estimates on contractors' calendars. Before I get into it — how are people finding you right now?"</dd>
<dt>You skip what they've tried before.</dt>
<dd>Ask it every time. A prospect burned by a previous agency has objections you can address before they surface, and you'll only know if you ask.</dd>
<dt>You quote a price and keep talking.</dt>
<dd>Say the number and stop. Practice the silence — it's a physical skill, and it's worth more than any script in this book.</dd>
<dt>You let the call end without a next date.</dt>
<dd>Open the calendar on the call. "I'll follow up" means it's over and neither of you has said so.</dd>
<dt>You never disqualify anybody.</dt>
<dd>If nobody fails your call, you aren't diagnosing. Taking a client whose arithmetic doesn't work costs you more than the revenue — it costs you the two months you spend failing to deliver.</dd>
<dt>You record without saying so.</dt>
<dd>Say the line. In Massachusetts this is a felony statute, not a formality, and it takes four seconds.</dd>
</dl>

## The metric

<div class="metric" markdown="1">
### What to track in this stage
**Show rate.** Booked calls that actually happened. If this sags, the problem is in the gap between booking and the call, not in the call itself — reminders, timing, how far out you're letting people schedule.

**Close rate.** Of calls that happened, how many bought.

Track both, always. Close rate alone rewards cherry-picking — a rep who only takes easy calls looks excellent and produces less. The number that matters is calls booked to deals closed, end to end.

**What "good" looks like:** improving against your own last twenty. And per Chapter 5, a close rate above half usually means your price is low, not that your calls are great.
</div>

<div class="donow" markdown="1">
### Do this now
<span class="time">35 minutes</span>
Write your opening frame — the 30-second version above, in your own words. Say it out loud until it doesn't sound read.

Then pick fifteen questions from the bank and put them in a document in the order you'll ask them. Not all of them. Fifteen you'll actually use.

Print it. Keep it next to you on every call for the next month. Reading questions off a page is completely fine and nobody can tell. Forgetting to ask what they've tried before is not fine, and you will forget, because you'll be nervous.
</div>
