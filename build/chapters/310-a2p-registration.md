<h1>Register Before You Build</h1>
<p class="chap-promise">By the end of this chapter you'll have messaging registration started on day one instead of discovering it in week three — and you'll know why that single sequencing decision protects the client relationship.</p>

Your own A2P 10DLC registration. What got rejected and why. How long it actually took versus what you expected. What you'd tell someone doing it Monday morning.</div>

This is the least exciting chapter in the book and probably the most valuable one.

Nobody makes content about carrier registration. There's no thumbnail in it. But it is the single most common reason a new agency's first client spends three weeks paying for silence — and a client who pays for three weeks of silence is a client who leaves in month two.

## The principle

<div class="principle"><span class="label">The principle</span><p>Registration runs on carrier time, not your time. It is the only part of the build you cannot compress, so it starts on day one — before the pipeline, before the automations, before anything.</p></div>

## Why most people get this wrong

They build in the order that feels natural, which is the order of visible progress.

Set up the account. Build the pipeline. Write the automations. Design the follow-up sequence. Test it — and discover the texts aren't going anywhere. Then start registration, which takes another week or two, during which the client is watching nothing happen and wondering what he's paying for.

The failure isn't technical. Everyone gets registered eventually. **The failure is sequencing**, and it costs you the most valuable asset you have with a new client: the first thirty days, when their patience is at its maximum and their doubt is at its minimum.

<p class="pull">Your client's goodwill is highest on day one and it depletes daily. Spending the first three weeks of it on a form you could have filed on day one is the most avoidable mistake in this business.</p>

## What A2P 10DLC actually is

Application-to-Person messaging over 10-digit long codes. In plain terms: when software sends a text from a normal-looking phone number, that's A2P. It's what your CRM does.

US carriers — AT&T, T-Mobile, Verizon — built a registration system to cut down spam. You register who you are and what you're going to send, and they decide whether your traffic gets through.

The part people miss: **since February 2025, unregistered A2P traffic is blocked outright.** Not slowed. Not delivered to spam. Blocked. Your messages leave your system, report as sent, and never arrive. You can look at a dashboard full of sent messages while the client's leads hear nothing.

Two things register, and they're separate:

**The brand** is the business. Legal name, EIN, address, contact. One per business.

**The campaign** is the use case. What kind of messages, sample copy, and how people opted in. One brand can have several campaigns.

## The system

### 1. Collect what you need before you open the form

Half of rejections come from mismatched details. The legal name and EIN have to match IRS records exactly — not the trade name, not the name on the truck.

<ul class="checklist">
<li>Legal business name, exactly as registered with the IRS</li>
<li>EIN, and the address on the EIN filing</li>
<li>Business type and industry</li>
<li>Website URL, live, with a visible privacy policy</li>
<li>Contact name, email on the business domain, and phone</li>
<li>Sample messages — the actual text you'll send</li>
<li>A description of how people opt in, plus a screenshot of the form</li>
</ul>

### 2. Fix the website first

The most common rejection cause is a website that doesn't back up the application.

The reviewer is checking that you are what you say you are. So the site needs to be live, to describe the business the campaign describes, and — this is the one people miss — to have a **privacy policy that specifically mentions SMS**, stating that mobile information isn't sold or shared with third parties for marketing.

If your client's opt-in is a web form, that form needs visible consent language next to the submit button. Not buried in terms. Next to the button.

### 3. Register the brand, then the campaign

Brand first. It's usually approved in **one to three business days**.

Then the campaign, which is the slower and pickier half: **three to seven business days**, and **ten to fifteen during busy periods.**

Campaign rejections almost always trace to one of three things: sample messages that don't match the stated use case, opt-in language that doesn't show real consent, or a use case description too vague to evaluate.

Write sample messages that are *actually representative*. If you'll send appointment reminders, the sample should be an appointment reminder with the placeholders filled in — not a generic greeting.

### 5. Say it out loud on the sales call

This is the move that turns a liability into an advantage.

On the discovery call, when you describe the build, tell them: *"First thing I do is get your messaging registered with the carriers. That takes about a week and it's out of my hands — but if we don't do it first, none of your texts get delivered. Most people find that out three weeks in."*

Two things happen. You've set the expectation before it can become a complaint. And you've demonstrated that you know something the last agency didn't — which does more for your credibility than any case study, especially given how many of your prospects have been burned before.

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
### Registration is not consent
Getting approved by the carriers does not give you permission to message anyone. Those are two separate things and people conflate them constantly.

Registration is the carriers agreeing to deliver your traffic. **Consent** is the recipient agreeing to receive it, and that's governed by the TCPA — which has moved twice recently and has another change scheduled. Chapter 17 covers the current state in detail.

Short version: you still need proper consent for every number you message, you still have to honor opt-outs promptly, and if a client hands you a list, find out how it was collected before you send anything. Their list becomes your problem the moment you press send.
</div>

## Failure modes

<dl class="failure" markdown="1">
<dt>You build everything else first.</dt>
<dd>Submit registration on day one, before you touch the pipeline. It runs in the background while you do the work you control.</dd>
<dt>Legal name doesn't match the EIN.</dt>
<dd>Ask for the EIN letter or a tax filing rather than the name on the truck. This single mismatch causes more rejections than everything else combined.</dd>
<dt>The privacy policy doesn't mention SMS.</dt>
<dd>Add the language before submitting. A site with no SMS clause is a predictable rejection and costs you a full week.</dd>
<dt>Sample messages don't match the use case.</dt>
<dd>Write the actual messages you'll send with placeholders filled in. Generic samples read as evasive and get bounced.</dd>
<dt>You promise a go-live date before approval lands.</dt>
<dd>Quote the range including the slow case, and tell the client the clock is the carriers', not yours. Under-promise here; you don't control this timeline.</dd>
<dt>You never test after approval.</dt>
<dd>Send a real message to your own phone. Check STOP and HELP work. Approved is not the same as working.</dd>
</dl>

## The metric

<div class="metric" markdown="1">
### What to track in this stage
**Days from signature to campaign approval.** Log it for every client. It's the first hard number in your delivery process and the one you can most reliably improve.

**Rejection rate and reason.** Track why. After three or four clients, the same reason will repeat, and it'll be something in your intake you can fix permanently.

**What "good" looks like:** submitted within one business day of signature, every time. Approval itself isn't yours to control — submission speed entirely is, and that's the number to hold yourself to.
</div>

<div class="donow" markdown="1">
### Do this now
<span class="time">40 minutes</span>
Register your own business first, before you ever do it for a client. You need to have felt the process.

If you have an EIN, start the brand registration today in whatever platform you're using. If you don't have one, apply — it's free and takes about ten minutes on the IRS site.

While that's processing, write your intake list: every piece of information you'll need to collect from a client on day one to submit theirs without a follow-up email. Put it in your onboarding form.

That list is worth more than the registration. It's what turns a three-week delay into a one-day submission for every client after this.
</div>
