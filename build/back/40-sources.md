### Research cited

Two studies are cited in this book. Both are named with their authors and dates so you can read them yourself, which you should — and both are old enough that you should weigh them accordingly.

**Lead response time.** James B. Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran and David Elkington, *"The Short Life of Online Sales Leads,"* Harvard Business Review 89, no. 3 (March 2011). An audit of 2,241 US companies. Firms attempting contact within an hour of an inquiry were roughly seven times more likely to qualify the lead than those trying an hour later, and more than sixty times more likely than those waiting a day. Cited in Chapter 12.

**Retention economics.** Frederick F. Reichheld and W. Earl Sasser Jr., *"Zero Defections: Quality Comes to Services,"* Harvard Business Review, September–October 1990. Found that reducing customer defections by 5% raised profits by 25% to 85%, with the largest gains in a bank branch system.

That second one is worth a note. You'll see this figure quoted almost everywhere as **25% to 95%.** It isn't. The original says 25% to 85%, and the higher number is a misquote that spread through marketing writing until it became the version people repeat. I've used the correct figure, and I'd encourage you to check any statistic you're about to repeat in your own marketing — including the ones in this book.

### On statistics generally

Several figures commonly cited in agency training — response-time averages, follow-up attempt counts, acquisition-versus-retention cost ratios — could not be traced to a locatable primary source when I went looking. I've left them out rather than repeat them.

If a number matters enough to put in front of a client, it matters enough to find the study.
